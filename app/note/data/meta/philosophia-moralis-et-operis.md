# Philosophia Moralis et Operis

> Reality is the total set of phenomena, entities, and processes that exist or occur, independently of an observer's perception or conceptualization, and which can, in principle, interact, change, or produce effects.

> Philosophia Moralis is the scaffolding used to deliberate, and render assessable, right action — the binding claims through which agents owe, judge, and answer to one another.

> This note systematizes a philosophy of the moral art that can serve as a conceptual foundation for organizing the ideas used to evaluate conduct across diverse domains. It is the complement of Philosophia Artium Technicarum et Operis (which systematizes intervention), Philosophia Artium Epistemicarum et Operis (which systematizes intelligibility), and Philosophia Socialium et Operis (which systematizes association): where those notes ask how reality is transformed, known, and coordinated, this one asks what ought to be done and who answers for it.

> Fiduciary stewardship is a training ground for rigorous moral modeling because it enforces observable, feedback-rich answerability under conflict: a steward holds another's good in trust, acts under explicit duties, and is judged on disclosure, loyalty, and care. The value lies in transferring this answerability discipline — rather than its specific codes — into more complex domains like polities and networked collectives.

## Formulation

### What is the nature of `moral practice`?

> Moral practice: (Reality; Agency, Claims) → Right Action & Answerability.

> A moral activity is any operation performed by an agent to deliberate among binding claims, act rightly under them, judge conduct as right or wrong, and answer for what was done.

> The goal of the moral art is (1) to construct and maintain the scaffolding mechanisms of deliberation and judgment, and (2) to stabilize the repertoires of right action — kept promises, disclosed truths, repaired breaches — through which agents remain worthy of trust.

### What is the nature of the `moral`?

> The moral is the assessability dimension of reality: the aspect under which an act can be right or wrong, permissible or impermissible, worthy of praise or blame — rendered tractable through the frame `M = <A,C,J,R>`: the Agent who acts, the Claims binding the act, the Judgment verdicting it, and the Responsibility answering for it.

### What is the role of `morality` in human experience?

> Morality enables agents to bind themselves across time and to one another: to make promises that hold, to hold goods in trust, to judge breaches without collapsing into vengeance, and to repair what wrongdoing breaks. Every promise kept, every truth disclosed against interest, every harm owned and repaired is the recursive coupling of freedom with answerability.

> The labour of moral practice goes hand in hand with a layout of reality — that is its deliberative template: every moral move presupposes a parsing of who owes what to whom under which claim, and every claim earns its keep through judged use.

### What grounds `moral practice`?

> Moral practice is grounded in the lived experience of being claimed by another: the entrusting look, the reliance already placed, the harm already risked — the pre-given horizon of mutual answerability within which every choice already finds itself. Agents (deliberative loci of choice), consciences (internal capacities for approval and self-accusation), and traditions (transmitted morphologies of precepts and exemplars) are crystallizations of this ground, not its foundation: they name how the claim–answer pairing stabilizes into recognizable offices and codes, never what makes the pairing possible.

### What is the reality we seek to act rightly within?

> Observer-independent reality as the substrate of answerability: agents, acts, consequences, goods at stake, and the claims — duties, rights, and warranted expectations — that bind agents to one another across that substrate. Reality is decomposed for deliberation in the decomposition-tree structure below.

### At which level of organization do we answer?

> The hierarchical stratification of choice and its reverberation, from punctual acts to formed character and shared orders — crucial when judging conduct with nested agency and multi-scale effects.

- **Micro-level:** Individual acts and their immediate claims.
- **Meso-level:** Offices, mandates, and communities of responsibility.
- **Macro-level:** Traditions, orders of right, settled character of collectives.

### What is the ontology of the target?

> What is reality like as assessable? Which agents, acts, claims, and judgments make it up? Ontology answers by constraining what can be owed and judged; its constructs for parsing (agents, acts, goods, principles, duties, judgments, responsibilities) live in the decomposition-tree structure below, read always against imputability and tradition.

### What Is a `Moral Element`?

> A Moral Element is anything that occupies a role in the practice by which conduct is rendered assessable: the agents who can be claimed; the acts owned; the goods at stake; the claims — principles, duties, rights, codes — that bind; the deliberations that weigh them; the judgments that verdict conduct; the responsibilities that answer for it; and the sanctions and repairs that restore it.

> Its boundaries are broad by design — a person, a promise, a duty, a verdict, and a stewardship compact sit in the same category — but they are not empty: an element qualifies when its absence, substitution, or alteration changes what can be owed, judged, or answered for, and the types order what it admits. The term denotes the category; the elements it covers are its instances.

> Moral elements are typed as kinds of the Moral Order and, when composite, decompose into further moral elements through explicitly typed relationships. A **`Moral Element Set`** is a coherent aggregate of them; a **`Moral Order`** is the set read as an order of right; a **`Moral Compound`** is a bounded ensemble (a compact, a community) that carries its own lanes; and a **`Moral Lane`** is one structural division of an order — the ten lanes below are the circuit an element travels when conduct is assessed.

>
> **Note:** This kind of structure neither completes nor normates a decomposition. The table states only which parent–child links are permitted; it decomposes nothing by itself, and it does not dictate what any particular element must decompose into. A decomposition is complete only for a bound root instance, grown link by permitted link until every leaf resolves to an instance.

> Note: Moral Element Type is recursive. The type of order of every moral element is the **Moral Order**. The `Permitted Link Forms` table below licenses how an element of one type decomposes into elements of another, and the **relationship table** names the connector that states each licensed relation. Template slots (`<<…>>`), where used, are bound with `:=` instead of instances. Relationships use `(→ Relationship Type)` throughout.

> **Note on lanes:** the ten lanes — `Moral Agency`, `Moral Goods`, `Moral Claim`, `Moral Action`, `Moral Deliberation`, `Moral Adjudication`, `Moral Answerability`, `Moral Transmission`, `Moral Dynamics`, `Moral Condition` — are structural element types, not tags. Each answers one question of the moral circuit: *who is claimed · what good · what claim · what act · how weighed · how judged · who answers · how handed down · what follows · under what standing.* A lane is reached by `Kind` from `Moral Element`, and its constituents by `Has` from the lane; an element is typed exactly once by the nearest enclosing lane.

> **Note on carrier types:** a `Moral Element Set` disaggregates by `Member`, never by `Part`; a `Moral Compound` disaggregates by `Part` and carries lanes by `Has`. A compound may contain compounds, which is what makes the type tree recursive.

> **Note on chains:** the act chain `Moral Agency → Moral Agent → Moral Action → Moral Act` and the normative chain `Moral Goods → Moral Claim → Moral Action` are lane traversals, not a separate template. An instance table may land at any depth of a chain; intermediate levels are grouping segments and are never skipped.

> **Note on imputability:** every moral element is read against the `Moral Condition` lane. **Capacity** holds whether the agent can deliberate and do otherwise; **Knowledge** holds what the agent could foresee; **Freedom** holds whether coercion, deception, or necessity defeats choice; **Stakes** holds what goods stand to be harmed or secured. The same name under defeated versus intact imputability denotes two readings, decomposed separately.

> **Note on traditions:** a tradition is an analytically distinct transmitted morphology defined by its precepts, exemplars, and casuistry (fiduciary care, oath-keeping, medical stewardship, civic republicanism…), not a container of agents. Traditions sit in the `Moral Transmission` lane and scope inquiry; they never own nodes.

> **Note on generic composites:** Different names may be used to refer to generic composites of moral elements, such as *Moral Domain*, *Moral Order*, *Ethical System*, or similar concepts. For simplicity we use **`Moral Compound`** as the generic ensemble type; an ensemble scope (a stewardship compact, a ward community) is typed `Moral Compound`. The composite `Community of Responsibility` heads compound nesting alongside it.

## How to decompose any moral instance?

> A decomposition recursively expands a moral instance tree from its root: bind the root instance, then grow its subtree by attaching only links the `Permitted Link Forms` table allows, down to instance leaves. The reference tables above are scaffolding — necessary but not sufficient, and never normative: they say which links *may* appear, not which decomposition is correct for any given element. Choosing instances, depth, and stopping point remains the decomposer's work on the instance itself.

> See the worked case in QA below (### (Case Study) What is the recursively decomposed instance tree of a Steward's Disclosure?). Read the case table as the worked in-path-typed tree: the empty table here is filled the same way, typing each row from its nearest enclosing grouping segment and naming every link with its relationship type.

> Begin with the condition test — capacity, knowledge, freedom, and stakes, the four constituents of the `Moral Condition` lane. Then trace the lanes: who is claimed, what good is at stake, what claim binds, what act follows, how it is weighed, how it is judged, who answers, what is handed down, what follows. Then grow the tree; an element typed by a lane never hangs loose from the root.

> Note: Instance rows in the Instance Tree Path name instances, never types. Type names appear only as backticked grouping segments and never terminate a branch.

> **Migration note:** the earlier Tabular view (nine moral categories over twenty-four element types), Recursive view (placeholder binding with spine expansion), and the first permitted-links draft (a flat star of `Kind` rows, one hop from the root) are replaced by the lane grammar above. Legacy rows remain readable because every old type survives as a lane constituent, but they are to be rewritten in this form when touched.

> **Constructing the instance path:** (1) bind the root — name the instance being decomposed; (2) traverse — reach its lane by `Kind`, then its lane constituent by `Has`; (3) extend — append each child by exactly one link the `Permitted Link Forms` table licenses, labelling it `(→ Relationship Type)`; (4) type — resolve each segment to its nearest enclosing grouping segment, declaring the root's type once; (5) check — every leaf resolves to an instance, no bare type terminates a branch, and no bare arrow remains.

**Rules:**

- **Form of the Table:** `Instance Tree Path | Description`.
- **Instance Tree Path**: A unique, recursively extended path identifying an instance within the decomposition tree. It begins at the root node `Moral Element`, descends the licensed chain through `Moral Element Set` and its kind to the instance's type, and appends each successive child through an explicitly labeled relationship, using the form Parent Instance (→ Relationship Type) Child Instance. Each successive path must preserve the complete path from the root to the current instance.
- **Root**: Every path begins at `Moral Element`. A whole-order root binds `Moral Element (→ Kind) Moral Element Set (→ Kind) Moral Order`; an ensemble root binds `Moral Element (→ Kind) Moral Element Set (→ Kind) Moral Compound`, takes its instance name, and carries its lanes beneath it.
- **Lane Traversal:** An element typed by a lane is reached `Moral Element (→ Kind) <Lane>` and `(→ Has) <Lane constituent>`. A lane may carry every constituent its slot licenses; it may not carry a constituent licensed only for another lane.
- **Relationship Labeling:** Every link between a parent and child names its relationship type, styled `(→ Kind)`, `(→ Member)`, `(→ Has)`, `(→ Instance)`, `(→ Binds)`, `(→ Assesses)`, `(→ Answers For)`, or another label from the relationship table. A bare arrow is not permitted. Typing reads from grouping segments, never from the link: an instance resolves to its nearest enclosing grouping segment's type, with the decomposition root's type declared once.
- **Grouping Segments:** Every backticked grouping segment names a type licensed by the `Permitted Link Forms` table or by a slot expansion. A type that is licensed by neither may not appear in a tree, even when the relationship itself is permitted.
- **One Type per Distinction:** Do not type the same distinction twice. A lane is a structural type, never also a tag; a set is disaggregated by `Member`, never by `Part`; a distinction already carried by one link is not re-asserted in the opposite direction by a second.
- **Permitted Links:** Every relationship must conform to a link form allowed by the `Permitted Link Forms` table. A link form licenses a possible relationship; it does not establish that the relationship actually exists in the instance being decomposed. `Instance` binds a type segment to an instance and needs no entry in that table.
- **Recursive Expansion:** Any moral instance identified in the tree may itself be decomposed recursively.
- **Decomposition Scope:** Select the aspects of the instance that need to be represented. A decomposition need not include every conceivable aspect of moral reality; it must adequately cover the aspects relevant to its stated purpose.
- **Stopping Condition:** Stop expanding a branch when its instance is sufficiently specified for the decomposition's purpose, when further expansion is unnecessary, or when the available evidence does not justify additional decomposition. A leaf is a stopping point in the current decomposition, not necessarily an intrinsically indivisible element. Every leaf must resolve to a moral instance.
- **Completeness:** A decomposition is complete relative to its declared scope and stopping criteria, not merely because every branch terminates. Relevant members, claims, relations, structures, processes, states, and other aspects must not be omitted simply because the tree is syntactically valid.
- **Unlicensed or Uncertain Relationships:** If a necessary relationship is not covered by the reference table, do not silently invent a permitted link or substitute an unrelated one. Identify the gap and extend the reference table when justified. If the existence of a relationship is uncertain, mark that uncertainty rather than presenting the relationship as established.
- **Instance Identity:** Distinguish different instances even when they share the same type or description. The full `Instance Tree Path` identifies an instance's position within the particular decomposition; it does not, by itself, establish a globally unique identity outside that tree.
- **Tree Structure:** Each non-root row must extend an existing parent path by exactly one child relationship. The same instance may appear in multiple branches when it plays different roles in the decomposition; each occurrence must have its own path. Ensure a rich set of intermediate (internal) nodes — both types and instances — aiding understanding: lane membership, binding, verdicting, answering, repairing, or other useful structural relationships.
- **Non-Normativity of Reference Tables:** The reference tables constrain which link forms may be used. They do not prescribe a universal decomposition order, determine which children must be included, or guarantee that a decomposition is conceptually adequate.
- **Well-formedness:** an instance decomposition is well-formed when every non-root row extends an existing path by exactly one link the `Permitted Link Forms` table licenses, every grouping segment carries a licensed type, lane membership is never skipped, and every leaf resolves to an instance. Any row may serve as a decomposition root for recursive decomposition.
- **Naming Guidelines:**
  * **Naming:** Name every instance node in Title Case — capitalize every whitespace- or hyphen-separated word (`harbor compact` -> `Harbor Compact`, `stage-entry criterion` -> `Stage-Entry Criterion`); preserve established all-caps acronyms (`API`, `EU`).
  * **Composite Instance Naming**: A *composite* instance — a nested ensemble (a `Moral Compound`, `Community of Responsibility`, or sub-domain set), not an ordinary agent, act, or judgment — takes a name ending in `Set` once it sits at a depth greater than 2. At depth 2 or less the suffix is permitted but never required.
- **Description:** Describe what the instance is, what role it plays, or why it is included in the decomposition. Descriptions clarify the instance but do not substitute for explicit relationship labels.

### How to decompose an instance that belongs to multiple element types?

> By default, a **multi-root forest**: one root per candidate type, each root growing its own well-formed tree. No instance row ever carries two types — typing reads from lanes and grouping segments, never from the row. Ambiguity is resolved by multiplication of trees, not by compromise typing.

> A moral element can belong to many types: a stewardship compact is a `Moral Compound` readable as a `Community of Responsibility`; a disclosure is a `Moral Act` readable as a `Duty / Obligation` discharged; a code is a `Precept / Code` readable as a `Tradition` fragment. Each reading gets its own root and its own tree: the Compact-as-Compound tree traverses lanes (agency, claim, action, adjudication), while the Compact-as-Community tree decomposes offices, mandates, and mutual answerability among members. Well-formedness per tree is unchanged — every link must be one the `Permitted Link Forms` table licenses; leaves are instances, intermediate nodes give structure.

> When the root typing is ambiguous, ask the user for disambiguation instead of guessing. If no answer comes, build the **default root**: a primary type chosen from the `Permitted Link Forms` table in Formulation (under "Which are some ideas to guide the decomposition of a `moral element`?"), recorded as the note's primary belonging in the "What moral element type does this moral instance belong to?" Formulation answer, with secondary readings kept as `readable as …` prose. The default root is therefore always explicit in the note itself.

## QA

### Where do promises and stewardships decompose?

> Promises traverse two lanes: in `Moral Agency` a `Moral Agent` (→ `Performs`) a `Moral Act`; in `Moral Claim` a `Duty / Obligation` (→ `Specifies`) from a `Principle / Maxim` (→ `Grounds`) by a `Value / Good`. The act (→ `Discharges`) the duty, is (→ `Assessed`) by `Moral Adjudication`, and is answered for in `Moral Answerability`. Stewardships are `Moral Compound`s carrying their own lanes: agency (stewards and their consciences), claim (charters, duties, rights of the ward), action (disclosures, care rounds), adjudication, and answerability (sanctions and repairs where trust breaks). Neither is single-condition — grow one tree per reading when imputability or typing is contested.

### What belongs to the social, technical, or epistemic instead?

> The coordination of roles and institutions belongs to the social decomposition even when morally loaded: decompose the institution socially, the obligation morally, and cross-link. The engineering of means belongs to the technical decomposition even when morally urgent: decompose the device technically, the choice to deploy it morally, and cross-link. The warranting of belief belongs to the epistemic decomposition even when beliefs excuse or condemn: decompose the artifact epistemically, the judgment morally, and cross-link. Edit the node where its grammar lives; view it wherever its role applies.

### What is the **most abstract formulation** that answerability can take?

- Claim → Deliberation → Responsible Act.
- Trust Placed → Choice Owned → Account Rendered.

### Why is the category `Moral Compound` required?

> A **Moral Compound** is required to represent coherent moral ensembles whose members belong to different moral lanes but are related through a common charge, trust, or answerability problem.

> Without this category, the taxonomy can describe the individual elements of such an ensemble, but it lacks a type for representing **the ensemble itself as a moral entity of organization** — one that carries its own lanes rather than merely aggregating members.

> **Take as example:** a harbor stewardship compact. It comprises stewards, wards, disclosures, care rounds, safety duties, rights of the ward, deliberations, judgments, and repairs. These elements sit in different lanes, but are related through the common scope of holding a harbor community's good in trust. The **Moral Compound** category provides a type for representing this coherent ensemble as a whole.

### Why shouldn't `Moral Knowledge` be a moral element type?

> Moral Knowledge should not be a moral-element type because knowledge is already represented by the taxonomy as a distinct ontological category (principles known, precedents studied, and the epistemic scaffolding that warrants them); making it a moral element would conflate the knowledge about a moral reality with the moral reality itself. What belongs here are the verdicts knowledge leaves behind: principles invoked, duties discharged, judgments rendered, repairs performed.

### Why do we need the lane `Moral Condition`?

> Because imputability is not a quality of the act or of the agent alone, but the standing under which an act counts as the agent's own. Carrying capacity, knowledge, freedom, and stakes as a lane makes the standing explicit and decomposable, so the same name under defeated versus intact imputability yields two readings instead of one equivocation. It is the moral analogue of the epistemic `Epistemic Context` lane: the surrounding conditions under which the practice is interpreted.

### Why are lanes types rather than tags?

> Because a tag routes nothing. The earlier vocabulary carried its nine categories in a `Tags` column, so every constituent still hung one hop from `Moral Element` and decompositions stayed two levels deep. Making the lanes structural types gives the grammar its middle: `Moral Element → Kind → Moral Element Set → Kind → Moral Compound → Has → Lane → Has → constituent → Instance`. Typing, licensing, and depth all follow from the lane the element belongs to.

### What Is a critical conceptual evaluation of the `Moral Element`?

> Evaluated is the definition given above, not its use: "A Moral Element is anything that occupies a role in the practice by which conduct is rendered assessable… An element qualifies when its absence, substitution, or alteration changes what can be owed, judged, or answered for. Moral elements are typed as kinds of the Moral Order and, when composite, decompose into further moral elements through explicitly typed relationships."

| Criterion | Verdict | Diagnostic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conceptual Specification** | | |
| Conceptual Identification | High | **Identity:** the differentia is stated — "occupies a role in the practice by which conduct is rendered assessable" — and the lane list (agency, goods, claim, action, deliberation, adjudication, answerability, transmission, dynamics, condition) marks the category off from its own lanes, which are positions inside it. |
| Definitional Clarity | Moderate–High | **Intelligibility:** the definiens is explicit and non-circular; the residual vagueness sits in "rendered assessable" and "can be owed, judged, or answered for", both of which require the practice to be named. |
| Conceptual Determinacy | Moderate–High | **Boundary rules:** the removal test decides borderline cases, and the lane grammar decides placement — a duty nobody could hold is out; a register entry in the action lane is in. |
| Internal Consistency | High | **Logical integrity:** membership does not run through the type system; lanes classify what the role test has already admitted, and decomposability follows membership instead of establishing it. |
| **Representational Adequacy** | | |
| Extensional Fidelity | Moderate–High | **Classification accuracy:** identical elements are admitted or refused by the role test; the extension of the lane constituents stays bound to the slot expansions. |
| Intensional Adequacy | High | **Defining properties:** the assessability practice and its lanes are the stated defining properties, not a proxy for them. |
| Ontological Adequacy | High | **Kind of entity:** it defines elements; the category/instance distinction is carried explicitly, and a compact enters as a composite element carrying lanes, never as a container of categories. |
| Discriminative Power | High | **Relevant distinctions:** the ten lanes separate who is claimed, what good, what claim, what act, how weighed, how judged, who answers, how transmitted, what follows, and under what standing. |
| Granularity Adequacy | High | **Level of abstraction:** lane membership is level-free, and recursion fixes granularity per purpose. |
| **Conceptual Economy** | | |
| Ontological Parsimony | Moderate | **Structural economy:** ten lane types and fourteen slots are more machinery than the loose original, but they replace a flat star of twenty-five `Kind` rows with a five-layer grammar. |
| Conceptual Distinctiveness | High | **Unique contribution:** lane-plus-role-plus-test is not the vocabulary of the neighbouring philosophiae. |
| Redundancy Control | High | **Conceptual overlap:** the lanes are disjoint divisions, so a constituent is typed once; imputability and transmission each do one job. |
| **Epistemic Performance** | | |
| Epistemic Leverage | High | **Analytical yield:** admission is a decision, placement is a lane, and both can be audited before decomposition begins. |
| Inferential Productivity | High | **Reasoning capacity:** lane membership licenses inference — if an act is judged, the claim it breached, the agent it binds, and the answerability it establishes are locatable lanes, not a search through a flat list. |
| Explanatory Power | Moderate–High | **Explanatory contribution:** it says why an element is moral and where it sits in the circuit that assesses conduct. |
| **Integration and Generality** | | |
| Contextual Robustness | High | **Contextual stability:** no conscience or inner-experience clause; the definition holds where judgment is distributed, institutional, or non-human. |
| Relational Compatibility | Moderate–High | **Compatibility:** compatible across the moral, social, technical, and epistemic orders by re-reading an element under a lane, though relation vocabulary remains unshared. |
| Scale Invariance | High | **Scale transfer:** lanes apply at every scale, and compounds carry their own lanes and recurse. |
| Temporal Stability | Moderate | **Historical continuity:** what counts as a claim, a wrong, or a repair is historically redefined; the lane structure survives the redefinition. |
| **Operational and Methodological Validity** | | |
| Operationalizability | High | **Procedural applicability:** run the condition test, pick the lane, name the constituent, then type and decompose. |
| Inter-Subjective Reproducibility | Moderate–High | **Agreement:** clear cases converge because the lane fixes the placement; a contested standing still needs a user question, which the guide already requires for ambiguous roots. |
| Falsifiability or Refutability | Moderate–High | **Exposure to criticism:** the test can fail — an element whose removal changes nothing owed, judged, or answered for is refused. |
| Purpose Adequacy | High | **Functional fit:** fit for admission, lane placement, typing, and decomposition in one instrument. |
| Misuse Resistance | Moderate | **Resistance to abuse:** the lane grammar blocks mere relabelling, though "assessable" can still be stretched by a determined user. |
