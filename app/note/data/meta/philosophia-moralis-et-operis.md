# Philosophia Moralis et Operis

> Reality is the total set of phenomena, entities, and processes that exist or occur, independently of an observer's perception or conceptualization, and which can, in principle, interact, change, or produce effects.

> Philosophia Moralis is the scaffolding used to deliberate, and render assessable, right action — the binding claims through which agents owe, judge, and answer to one another.

> This note systematizes a philosophy of the moral art that can serve as a conceptual foundation for organizing the ideas used to evaluate conduct across diverse domains. It is the complement of Philosophia Artium Technicarum et Operis (which systematizes intervention), Philosophia Artium Epistemicarum et Operis (which systematizes intelligibility), and Philosophia Socialium et Operis (which systematizes association): where those notes ask how reality is transformed, known, and coordinated, this one asks what ought to be done and who answers for it.

> Fiduciary stewardship is a training ground for rigorous moral modeling because it enforces observable, feedback-rich answerability under conflict: a steward holds another's good in trust, acts under explicit duties, and is judged on disclosure, loyalty, and care. The value lies in transferring this answerability discipline — rather than its specific codes — into more complex domains like polities and networked collectives.

## Formulation

### What is the nature of `moral practice`?

> Moral practice: (Reality; Agency, Claims) → Right Action & Answerability.

> A moral activity is any operation performed by an agent to deliberate among binding claims, act rightly under them, judge conduct as right or wrong, and answer for what was done.

> The goal of the moral art is (1) to construct and maintain the scaffolding mechanisms of deliberation and judgment, and (2) to stabilize the repertoires of right action — kept promises, disclosed truths, repaired breaches — through which agents remain worthy of trust.

### What is the nature of the `moral`?

> The moral is the assessability dimension of reality: the aspect under which an act can be right or wrong, permissible or impermissible, worthy of praise or blame — rendered tractable through the frame `M = <A,C,J,R>`: the Agent who acts, the Claims binding the act, the Judgment verdicting it, and the Responsibility answering for it.

### What is the role of `morality` in human experience?

> Morality enables agents to bind themselves across time and to one another: to make promises that hold, to hold goods in trust, to judge breaches without collapsing into vengeance, and to repair what wrongdoing breaks. Every promise kept, every truth disclosed against interest, every harm owned and repaired is the recursive coupling of freedom with answerability.

> The labour of moral practice goes hand in hand with a layout of reality — that is its deliberative template: every moral move presupposes a parsing of who owes what to whom under which claim, and every claim earns its keep through judged use.

### What is a `moral element`?

> It is a concept that denotes an element participating in the deliberation-oriented and answerability-oriented dimension of conduct experienced by humans or other purposeful agents. Note: This is a deliberately loose definition of the reality denoted by the concept.

> Foundation - **Reality -** The ontological substrate comprising the agents, acts, consequences, goods, and binding claims that constrain and respond to moral action. Person, promise, trust fund,commons, patient, mandate.

### What grounds `moral practice`?

> Moral practice is grounded in the lived experience of being claimed by another: the entrusting look, the reliance already placed, the harm already risked — the pre-given horizon of mutual answerability within which every choice already finds itself. Agents (deliberative loci of choice), consciences (internal capacities for approval and self-accusation), and traditions (transmitted morphologies of precepts and exemplars) are crystallizations of this ground, not its foundation: they name how the claim–answer pairing stabilizes into recognizable offices and codes, never what makes the pairing possible.

### What is the reality we seek to act rightly within?

> Observer-independent reality as the substrate of answerability: agents, acts, consequences, goods at stake, and the claims — duties, rights, and warranted expectations — that bind agents to one another across that substrate. Reality is decomposed for deliberation in the Moral Order tree below.

### At which level of organization do we answer?

> The hierarchical stratification of choice and its reverberation, from punctual acts to formed character and shared orders — crucial when judging conduct with nested agency and multi-scale effects.

- **Micro-level:** Individual acts and their immediate claims.
- **Meso-level:** Offices, mandates, and communities of responsibility.
- **Macro-level:** Traditions, orders of right, settled character of collectives.

### What is the ontology of the target?

> What is reality like as assessable? Which agents, acts, claims, and judgments make it up? Ontology answers by constraining what can be owed and judged; its constructs for parsing (agents, acts, goods, principles, duties, judgments, responsibilities) live in the Moral Order tree below, read always against imputability and tradition.

### How can we characterize the moral aspect of human experience?

> A taxonomy (conceptual structure) that renders the *deliberation-oriented* and *answerability-oriented* practice of agents intelligible.

> The labour of moral practice goes hand in hand with a layout of reality — that is its deliberative template: every act presupposes a parsing of who owes what to whom, and every claim earns its keep through judged use.

> Note: Moral Element Type is recursive. The single table below holds the branch: `(root) := <<Moral Element>> -> Moral Order` (agents, acts, goods, norms, judgments, responsibilities, compounds, dynamics). Each branch root carries its path and child rows extend it; child descriptions state the role-in-parent, canonical definitions live on root rows. Scaffold rows are those supporting, structuring, or verdicting action — deliberations, principles, judgments, traditions, states, responsibilities, repairs; the rest are morality's agents, acts, and goods.

> **Note on imputability:** every moral element is read against four conditions. **Capacity** holds whether the agent can deliberate and do otherwise; **Knowledge** holds what the agent could foresee; **Freedom** holds whether coercion, deception, or necessity defeats choice; **Stakes** holds what goods stand to be harmed or secured. The same name under defeated versus intact imputability denotes two readings, decomposed separately.

> **Note on traditions:** a tradition is an analytically distinct transmitted morphology defined by its precepts, exemplars, and casuistry (fiduciary care, oath-keeping, medical stewardship, civic republicanism…), not a container of agents. Traditions scope inquiry; they never own nodes.

> **Note on generic composites:** Different names may be used to refer to generic composites of moral elements, such as *Moral Domain*, *Moral Order*, *Ethical System*, or similar concepts. For simplicity we use **`Moral Compound`** as the generic type; ensemble scopes (stewardship compacts, ward communities) bind the placeholder as `Moral Compound`. The composite `Community of Responsibility` heads compound nesting alongside it.

> Recursion is introduced by allowing a composite to have another composite as an instance: a `Moral Compound` may contain `Moral Compound`s (a ward-care compact within a harbor stewardship compact). Nesting composites in composites is what makes the type tree recursive.

> **Spines (adapted to the moral):** act-spine `Moral Agent -> Conscience -> Moral Act -> Practice / Habit`; normative-spine `Value / Good -> Principle / Maxim -> Duty / Obligation -> Right`. Flat judgment types land directly under the bound root element. An instance table may land at any depth of a spine; intermediate levels are structuring types, never skipped.

## How to decompose any moral instance?

> A decomposition of a moral instance expands its tree from the root — the conduct under assessment — adding the agents, acts, goods, principles, duties, judgments, and responsibilities that bind, move, verdict, and answer for it.

> Begin with the imputability test: does the agent have capacity, knowledge, and freedom, and what stakes ride on the act? Then assign its tradition by governing precepts and exemplars, never by location or actors. Then grow the tree.

> See the worked case in QA below (### (Case Study) What is the recursively decomposed instance tree of a Steward's Disclosure?). Read the case table as the worked in-path-typed tree: the empty table here is filled the same way, typing each row from its grouping segments and the declared root binding.

> **Moral Element Type Tree Path:** the expansion path typing one instance row — `(root) := <<Moral Element>>` bound to the row's table type, continued by exactly one licensed expansion: a spine-ordered suffix, a tradition attachment, or a composite nesting. In other words, a Tree Path is what the single table generates. It is a type-level path (`->`), never to be confused with the Instance Tree Path (first column), which strings instances with `→`.

> **Constructing the path:** (1) type the instance — find its row in the table above; (2) bind — write `(root) := <<Moral Element>>` as that type; (3) extend — continue with exactly one licensed expansion from the bound position (spine-ordered suffix, tradition attachment under the bound root, or composite nesting); (4) check — single types and spine-ordered chains always license; anything else must match a composite nesting.

The tree is governed by the following rules:

* **Root:** The root is the moral instance being decomposed.
* **Structure:** Intermediate nodes provide the structure needed to organize the decomposition - they can be moral instances - or moral types. Moral Types cannot be the final nodes in the tree - may be used as grouping nodes, but they are not themselves instances.
* **Leaves:** Every leaf must resolve to a moral instance.
* **Typing:** Every instance is typed — by its nearest enclosing grouping segment, with the decomposition root's type declared once.
* **Recursion:** Any instance in the tree may itself be decomposed recursively.
* **Well-formedness:** an instance decomposition is well-formed when every instance row's type path is a path the table generates: bind `(root) := <<Moral Element>>` to the root instance's type and continue down-branch in order; branch order is never skipped. Any row may serve as a decomposition root for recursive decomposition.
* **No repetition:** The root's own type must not be unnecessarily repeated as an intermediate grouping node. Exception: a same-type segment is allowed when it scopes a genuine instance family that would otherwise hang untyped (e.g. a `Duty / Obligation` grouping scoping the disclosure-duty family inside a Stewardship decomposition, whose counterpart is `(root) := <<Moral Element>> -> ... -> Duty / Obligation`); a same-type segment with only generic description and no scoping function stays forbidden.
* **Well-Form Instance Tree Path Rule:** Ensure the decomposition provides a rich set of intermediate (internals) nodes - both - type and instances, aiding understanding. The set of intermediate instance nodes representing relationships such as binding, grounding, verdicting, answering, repairing, or other useful structural and dynamic relationships.
* Style Rules for Intermediate Nodes
  * **Instances:** Style intermediate nodes that represent actual moral instances as plain text (no adornment).
  * **Naming:** Name every instance node in Title Case — capitalize every whitespace- or hyphen-separated word (`harbor compact` -> `Harbor Compact`, `stage-entry criterion` -> `Stage-Entry Criterion`); preserve established all-caps acronyms (`API`, `EU`). Type segments keep their table casing. The rule governs node names in Instance Tree Paths only; descriptions stay sentence-case prose.
  * **Composite Instance Naming**: A *composite* instance — a nested ensemble (a `Moral Compound`, `Community of Responsibility`, or sub-domain set), not an ordinary agent, act, or judgment — takes a name ending in `Set` once it sits at a depth greater than 2. Depth counts every segment of the Instance Tree Path, backticked type groupings included, the root type grouping being depth 1. At depth 2 or less the suffix is permitted but never required: `Moral Compound` -> `Harbor Stewardship Compact Set` (depth 2) keeps it, `Moral Compound` -> `Ward-Care Compact Set` (depth 3) requires it.
  * **Types:** Style bare moral element types used as grouping nodes as `` `code` ``.
  * **Distinction:** Never style an instance and a type in the same way; the distinction must be immediately visible.
  * **Grouping types:** A type used only to group instances is not itself an instance and must not terminate a branch.
  * The path link - is →.
* **Moral Element Type Tree Path**: In the definition table this column is a template carrying the `<<Moral Element>>` root slot; once the root instance's type is bound (`(root) := …`), the instantiated path contains only concrete moral element types — no expansion patterns or placeholders.

### How to decompose an instance that belongs to multiple element types?

> By default, a **multi-root forest**: one root per candidate type, each root growing its own well-formed tree. No instance row ever carries two types — typing reads from grouping segments and the declared root binding. Ambiguity is resolved by multiplication of trees, not by compromise typing.

> A moral element can belong to many types: a stewardship compact is a `Moral Compound` readable as a `Community of Responsibility`; a disclosure is a `Moral Act` readable as a `Duty / Obligation` discharged; a code is a `Precept / Code` readable as a `Tradition` fragment. Each reading gets its own root and its own tree: the Compact-as-Compound tree decomposes agents, acts, and judgments (ensemble members), while the Compact-as-Community tree decomposes offices, mandates, and mutual answerability (membership content). Well-formedness per tree is unchanged — every path must be a path the single table generates; leaves are instances, intermediate nodes give structure.

> When the root typing is ambiguous, ask the user for disambiguation instead of guessing. If no answer comes, build the **default root**: a primary type chosen from the table above (the "How can we characterize the moral aspect of human experience?" table), recorded as the note's primary belonging in the "What moral element type does this moral instance belong to?" Formulation answer, with secondary readings kept as `readable as …` prose. The default root is therefore always explicit in the note itself.

## QA

### Where do promises and stewardships decompose?

> Promises decompose as `Moral Act`s binding promisor to promisee under a `Duty / Obligation` grounded in a `Principle / Maxim` (fidelity), verdict by `Moral Judgment` and held by `Responsibility / Accountability`. Stewardships decompose as `Moral Compound`s: `Moral Agent`s with `Conscience`, `Moral Act`s of disclosure and care, `Value / Good`s at stake, and `Sanction / Repair` where trust breaks. Neither is single-condition — grow one tree per reading when imputability or typing is contested.

### What belongs to the social, technical, or epistemic instead?

> The coordination of roles and institutions belongs to the social decomposition even when morally loaded: decompose the institution socially, the obligation morally, and cross-link. The engineering of means belongs to the technical decomposition even when morally urgent: decompose the device technically, the choice to deploy it morally, and cross-link. The warranting of belief belongs to the epistemic decomposition even when beliefs excuse or condemn: decompose the artifact epistemically, the judgment morally, and cross-link. Edit the node where its grammar lives; view it wherever its role applies.

### What is the **most abstract formulation** that answerability can take?

- Claim → Deliberation → Responsible Act.
- Trust Placed → Choice Owned → Account Rendered.

### Why is the category `Moral Compound` required?

> A **Moral Compound** is required to represent coherent moral ensembles whose members belong to different moral element types but are related through a common charge, trust, or answerability problem.

> Without this category, the taxonomy can describe the individual elements of such an ensemble, but it lacks a type for representing **the ensemble itself as a moral entity of organization**.

> **Take as example:** a harbor stewardship compact. It comprises stewards, wards, disclosures, care rounds, safety duties, rights of the ward, deliberations, judgments, and repairs. These elements have different moral element types, but are related through the common scope of holding a harbor community's good in trust. The **Moral Compound** category provides a type for representing this coherent ensemble as a whole.

### Why shouldn't `Moral Knowledge` be a moral element type?

> Moral Knowledge should not be a moral-element type because knowledge is already represented by the taxonomy as a distinct ontological category (principles known, precedents studied, and the epistemic scaffolding that warrants them); making it a moral element would conflate the knowledge about a moral reality with the moral reality itself. What belongs here are the verdicts knowledge leaves behind: principles invoked, duties discharged, judgments rendered, repairs performed.

### Why do we need imputability and traditions?

> Imputability (capacity, knowledge, freedom, stakes) and traditions fill the ontological gap between **conduct itself** and the **decomposition that renders it assessable**. Imputability states under which standing an act counts as the agent's own — intact or defeated — so the same name under two standings decomposes as two readings. Traditions state by which transmitted morphology of precepts and exemplars it is scoped — fiduciary care, oath-keeping, civic stewardship — so inquiry stays scoped without owning nodes. Together they provide the standing-plus-morphology structure against which moral elements are typed, deliberated, judged, and answered for.
