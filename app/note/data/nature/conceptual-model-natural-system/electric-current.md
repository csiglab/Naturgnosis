# Electric Current

> **Electric Current** is defined as the **rate of flow of electric charge** through a **conductive medium** per unit time.
>

## Index

## Formulation

Mathematically, it is expressed as:  $I=\frac{dQ}{dt}$

where:

- $I$ = Electric current (measured in **amperes, A**),
- $Q$ = Electric charge (measured in **coulombs, C**),
- $t$ = Time (measured in **seconds, s**).

## Measure

> Which physical quantities are used to measure electric current?
>

| Measure | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Current (I)** | The flow of electric charge per unit time, measured in **amperes (A)**. |
| **Voltage (V)** | The electric potential difference that drives the current, measured in **volts (V)**. Useful indirectly to calculate current via Ohm’s law. |
| **Resistance (R)** | Opposition to the flow of current, measured in **ohms (Ω)**. Also used indirectly to determine current. |
| **Conductance (G)** | The ease with which current flows, reciprocal of resistance, measured in **siemens (S)**. |
| **Charge (Q)** | Total electric charge passed, measured in **coulombs (C)**. Current can be derived as **I = dQ/dt**. |
| **Power (P)** | Electric power consumed or delivered, measured in **watts (W)**; can be used with voltage to calculate current: **I = P/V**. |

### Current

> Which are it’s denominations?
>

| Denomination | Symbol | Value | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ampere** | A | Base SI unit | Standard unit of electric current. |
| **Milliampere** | mA | 1 mA = 10⁻³ A | 1 thousandth of an ampere, often used for small currents. |
| **Microampere** | μA | 1 μA = 10⁻⁶ A | 1 millionth of an ampere, for very small currents. |
| **Kiloampere** | kA | 1 kA = 10³ A | 1 thousand amperes, used for industrial or high-power currents. |
| **Centiampere** | cA | 1 cA = 10⁻² A | Rarely used, 1 hundredth of an ampere. |
| **Nanoampere** | nA | 1 nA = 10⁻⁹ A | Extremely small currents, often in electronics and sensors. |
| **Picoampere** | pA | 1 pA = 10⁻¹² A | Ultra-small currents, e.g., in microelectronics or photodetectors. |

## Taxonomy

| **Type of Current** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct Current (DC)** | Electric current that flows in a single, constant direction; magnitude may be steady or slowly varying. Common in batteries and electronics. |
| **Alternating Current (AC)** | Electric current that periodically reverses direction; the magnitude often varies sinusoidally. Used in power grids and household electricity. |
| **Pulsating DC** | A unidirectional current whose magnitude varies with time, often produced by rectifying AC. It never reverses direction but fluctuates in strength. |
| **Sinusoidal AC** | Alternating current whose magnitude varies as a smooth sine wave over time; the most common form of AC in power distribution. |
| **Square Wave AC** | Alternating current that switches abruptly between two levels (high and low), producing a square waveform. Often used in digital circuits and pulse testing. |
| **Triangular Wave AC** | Alternating current with a waveform that linearly ramps up and down, forming a triangular shape; used in signal processing and testing. |
| **Pulsed Current** | Current delivered in short bursts or pulses, with periods of zero current in between; used in welding, medical devices, and communication systems. |
| **High-Frequency AC** | Alternating current with a very high frequency (kHz to MHz), often used in radio transmission, induction heating, and resonant circuits. |
| **Three-Phase AC** | AC system with three sinusoidal currents of equal frequency, each phase shifted by 120°, providing more efficient power transmission. |
| **Single-Phase AC** | AC system with a single sinusoidal voltage; simpler than three-phase, commonly used in residential power supply. |

## Movement

> What underlie the movement of charges?
>

| Factor | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Electric Field** | A force exerted on charges due to a potential difference. Causes charges to accelerate in the direction of the field (for positive charges) or opposite (for negative charges). |
| **Magnetic Field** | A force acts on **moving charges** perpendicular to their velocity and the magnetic field direction (Lorentz force). This can change the direction of motion without changing speed. |
| **Potential Difference / Voltage** | A source of energy that creates an electric field; drives continuous movement of charges in a circuit. |
| **Conductive Path** | A medium (wire, plasma, electrolyte) that allows charges to move freely. Resistance of the path affects the current. |
| **Force Imbalance** | Charges move when net forces act on them. Without a net force, charges remain stationary (ignoring thermal motion). |
| **External Energy Source** | Batteries, generators, or other energy sources provide the energy to sustain movement of charges over time. |

## Continuous Flow of Charge

> What is needed for the Continuous Flow of Charge?
>

| Condition | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Closed Circuit** | Charges need a complete path to return to the source; without it, current cannot be sustained. |
| **Potential Difference (Voltage)** | Provides the driving force (electric field) that pushes charges through the circuit. |
| **Conductive Path** | A medium (wire, electrolyte, or conductor) that allows charges to move with minimal obstruction. |
| **Energy Source** | Batteries, generators, or other devices supply the energy required to maintain continuous flow. |
| **Low-Resistance Path** | Reduces energy loss and allows more efficient flow of charges; too high resistance can stop or limit current. |
