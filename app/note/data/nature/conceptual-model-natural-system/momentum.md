# Momentum

> **Momentum** is an abstraction that **characterize the motion** of an object; basically velocity and mass.
>

> **Momentum** is a physical quantity that describes the motion of an object, defined as the product of its **mass and velocity**.
>

> The **abstraction of momentum** is useful because it simplifies the analysis of motion and interactions in physics, allowing for the formulation of fundamental principles such as the conservation of momentum, which applies across various systems (e.g., collisions, particle dynamics, and even fluid dynamics) regardless of the specific details of the forces involved. This abstraction provides a powerful tool for predicting outcomes, solving complex problems, and establishing universal laws that govern both macroscopic and microscopic phenomena.
>

## History of the Term

> In the history of physics, momentum has been referred to by several different names, often depending on the context in which it was being discussed.
>

Some of the older names or terms associated with momentum include:

1. **Quantity of Motion**: This is one of the earliest terms used to describe momentum. Sir Isaac Newton referred to momentum as "the quantity of motion" in his work *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687), where he formulated his laws of motion.
2. **Vis Viva (Living Force)**: This term was used by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the 17th century. "Vis viva" referred to the energy of a moving body, which he described as being proportional to the square of its velocity. This concept laid the groundwork for the modern notion of kinetic energy, and although distinct, it was related to momentum in terms of motion.
3. **Impulse**: Though now used to refer to a change in momentum (force applied over time), the term "impulse" was historically sometimes used in the context of momentum itself. The concept of impulse as force × time and its connection to momentum became clearer in the 19th century.
4. **Action**: In the early days of mechanics, "action" was a term used to describe the product of mass and velocity (similar to momentum). It was often used in the context of the principle of least action in variational mechanics, where the action is minimized during a system's motion.

## Specific Formulation

| Formulation | **Definition** | **Context** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linear Momentum** | Quantity of motion of a moving body | Classical mechanics |
| **Angular Momentum** | Rotational equivalent of linear momentum | Rotational dynamics |
| **Canonical Momentum** | Generalized momentum in Lagrangian/Hamiltonian mechanics | Analytical mechanics |
| **Relativistic Momentum** | Linear momentum adjusted for relativistic effects | Special relativity |
| **Electromagnetic Momentum** | Momentum carried by electromagnetic fields | Electrodynamics |
| **Conjugate Momentum** | Momentum conjugate to a coordinate in Lagrangian systems | Field theory, mechanics |
| **Mechanical Momentum** | Total of all momenta in a mechanical system | Systems of particles |
| **Spin Angular Momentum** | Intrinsic angular momentum of quantum particles | Quantum mechanics |
| **Orbital Angular Momentum** | Angular momentum due to spatial motion of a particle | Atomic/quantum physics |
| **Generalized Momentum** | Momentum derived from generalized coordinates in mechanics | Lagrangian/Hamiltonian formalism |
| **Fluid Momentum** | Momentum in fluid flow (bulk or local) | Fluid mechanics |
| **Photon Momentum** | Momentum of massless particles like photons | Quantum electrodynamics |
| **Impulse (Change in Momentum)** | Change in momentum due to a force acting over time | Mechanics |
