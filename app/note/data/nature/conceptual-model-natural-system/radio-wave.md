# Radio Wave

> In this note, we will explore the interesting phenomenon called a radio wave.
>

> A **radio wave**, as a phenomenon, arises as a pattern of perturbations in the electromagnetic fields that propagates through space.
>

> Which are the missing questions that we should be asking about radio waves?
>

> Note: A wave is a complex concept—it requires us to think in terms of field perturbations, measure those perturbations, and then assign the resulting pattern to an entity called a “wave.” Things become complicated when making this attributions.
>

> The phenomenon of a wave is part of a larger class of phenomena called radiation.
>

## Formulation

### What is the nature of a radio wave?

> A **radio wave** is a **coherent disturbance (perturbation) in the electromagnetic field that propagates through space**, carrying energy and potentially information.
>

A **radio wave** is:

- A **coherent perturbation** of the electromagnetic field (electric **E** and magnetic **B** fields)
- That **propagates through space** at a speed determined by the medium (≈ c in vacuum)
- **Carries energy** (proportional to the square of the field amplitude)
- **Can carry information** through modulation of amplitude, frequency, phase, or polarization

Essentially, it’s a **moving pattern of field disturbances**, not a substance, but a dynamic configuration of electromagnetic energy.

### What is the ontic nature  of a radio wave?

The **ontic (being) nature** asks what it *is* rather than how we describe it:

- A radio wave **exists as a spatial-temporal structure** of the electromagnetic field.
- It is **real insofar as its effects are observable**: it can impart energy to charges (e.g., antenna currents), produce interference, or be absorbed by materials.
- It is **quantifiable** in terms of field vectors, frequency, wavelength, polarization, and amplitude.
- Fundamentally, it’s a **field perturbation constrained by Maxwell’s equations**, with energy and momentum.

### How to **identify (identity)** a wave?

Since  a wave is a ‘pattern of perturbation’ -

A wave is identified by observing its characteristic properties:

- **Oscillatory behavior**: Repetition in time or space (frequency, wavelength)
- **Propagation**: Movement of the disturbance through a medium or vacuum
- **Coherence**: Predictable phase relationship across time or space
- **Interaction with detectors**: Induces currents in antennas or measurable forces on charges
- **Modulation patterns**: Encodes information that can be demodulated

So, a wave’s identity comes from **its combination of oscillation, coherence, and interaction behavior**.

### How to measure - a radio wave?

Measurement depends on what property you want to observe:

- **Frequency / Wavelength** → Spectrum analyzers, oscilloscopes
- **Amplitude / Intensity** → Power meters, antennas, field probes
- **Phase / Coherence** → Interferometry, phase detectors
- **Polarization** → Antenna orientation, polarization filters
- **Direction / Displacement** → Directional antennas, phased arrays, time-of-arrival methods
- **Propagation effects** → Received signal strength vs. distance, reflection/refraction patterns

Each measurement is a mapping from the **wave’s physical manifestation to a quantitative representation**.

### What makes an electro-magnetic wave a radio wave?

A radio wave is defined **by its frequency range**, not its ontic structure:

- **Radio spectrum**: ~3 kHz to 300 GHz (wavelength ~100 km to 1 mm)
- Any electromagnetic wave within this range qualifies as a radio wave
- Its **practical distinction** comes from propagation and technological usage: e.g., wireless communication, broadcasting, radar

So, an EM wave **becomes a radio wave** when it falls within the radio frequency bands used for communication and observation.

### What gives origins (source) (physical origin -not emitter) to a radio wave?

> What do we mean by origin, origin as emitter, origin as some sort of physical substrate type generating the wave?
>

> The EM field perturbation **does not arise “by itself”** — it is a consequence of **(**the **origins of the** perturbation**).**
>

| Category | Physical Origin Type | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accelerating Electric Charges** |  | Time-varying motion of charges (typically electrons) creates oscillating electric and magnetic fields, which propagate as EM waves. This is the fundamental microscopic source of all radio waves. |
| **Time-Varying Electric Current in Conductors** |  | Oscillating currents in wires, circuits, or antennas generate changing magnetic fields and electric fields, which form the wave at a macroscopic scale. |
| **Electromagnetic Field Perturbation** |  | Local fluctuations in the $(\mathbf{E})$ and $(\mathbf{B})$ fields act as the medium of the wave; the wave is the propagation of this perturbation through space. |
| **Plasma or Charge Separation Oscillations** |  | In certain media (e.g., ionosphere or plasma antennas), collective oscillations of charges produce EM waves. |
| **Resonant Structures (e.g., cavity, LC circuits)** |  | Physical systems with natural oscillation modes can sustain and radiate EM field variations; the oscillating field pattern serves as the wave’s physical origin. |

## Affirmation Space

What is the space of affirmation (things that can be said) types about radio waves?

- **Intrinsic properties** (frequency, wavelength, amplitude, phase, polarization).
- **How it propagates** (modes, reflection, refraction, diffraction, dispersion).
- **Effects on or by matter** (attenuation, coupling, resonance, interaction).
- **Signal-specific attributes** (modulation, coherence, interference).
- **Fundamental quantum nature** (photon energy).
- **Perturbations or noise** (environmental, thermal, cosmic).

| **Category** | **Affirmation Type** | **Description** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source** | Origin | Where and how the wave is generated (antenna type, emission pattern, power, location). |
| **Physical Property** | Frequency | Number of oscillations per second of the electromagnetic field, measured in Hz. Determines energy and propagation characteristics. |
|  | Wavelength | Distance over which the wave’s shape repeats, λ = c/f. Inversely proportional to frequency. |
|  | Amplitude / Intensity | Magnitude of the electric or magnetic field; relates to signal strength or power. |
|  | Phase | Relative position of the wave in its cycle at a given point; important for interference and coherent detection. |
|  | Polarization | Orientation of the electric field vector; can be linear, circular, or elliptical. |
| **Propagation Phenomenon** | Propagation Mode | Path by which waves travel: line-of-sight, ground wave, sky wave (ionospheric reflection), or guided modes. |
|  | Reflection | Bouncing of waves off surfaces; affects multipath and coverage. |
|  | Refraction | Bending of waves when crossing media with different refractive indices (e.g., ionosphere, atmosphere). |
|  | Diffraction (Bending) | Bending of waves around obstacles or edges; enables reception beyond line-of-sight. |
|  | Dispersion (Group Velocity) | Variation of wave velocity with frequency; can spread pulse signals in time. |
| **Interaction (Effect)** | Interaction with Matter | Absorption, scattering, or emission of radio waves by materials; depends on frequency and medium. |
|  | Attenuation / Loss | Reduction in signal amplitude due to distance, medium, or obstacles. |
|  | Coupling / Transmission | Transfer of energy between antennas, circuits, or media; affects efficiency. |
|  | Resonance / Standing Waves | Formation of stationary patterns due to constructive/destructive interference; relevant in cavities and antennas. |
| **Signal Property** | Modulation / Encoding | Variation of amplitude, frequency, or phase to encode information (AM, FM, digital schemes). |
|  | Coherence | Measure of phase correlation over time or space; important for interferometry and radar. |
|  | Interference / Superposition | Overlapping of multiple waves, leading to constructive or destructive effects. |
| **Quantum** | Quantum Nature / Photon Energy | Energy of individual quanta of the electromagnetic field, E = hf; relevant at very high frequencies. |
| **Perturbation** | Noise / Perturbation | Random or unwanted variations (thermal, atmospheric, cosmic) affecting signal clarity. |

### Formalism

> Which concrete representations (formalisms) are used in practice?
>

| **Formalism** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Maxwell’s Equations (Differential Form)** | Fundamental equations describing electric **E** and magnetic **B** fields: $(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J})$. Used to model wave propagation, sources, and boundary interactions. |
| **Wave Equation** | Derived from Maxwell’s equations: $(\nabla^2 \mathbf{E} - \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0)$. Practical for plane wave, spherical wave, and pulse analysis. |
| **Poynting Vector** | $(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H})$; represents energy flux density. Used in power calculations, antenna radiation, and propagation studies. |
| **Complex Phasor Representation** | $(\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) = \Re{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r}) e^{j\omega t}})$. Used for sinusoidal steady-state analysis, impedance calculations, and AC circuit coupling. |
| **Fourier (Spectral Representation)** | $(E(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} E(t)e^{-i 2 \pi f t} dt)$. Decomposes signals into frequency components; critical for modulation, bandwidth analysis, and communication system design. |
| **Antenna / Network Models** | Equivalent circuits (RLC, transmission lines) and radiation patterns; map EM field to measurable currents; used in practical RF engineering. |
| **Numerical Methods (FDTD, FEM, Method of Moments)** | Discrete computational models to simulate propagation, reflection, diffraction, scattering. Used in simulation and design of antennas, radar, and communication systems. |
| **Ray Optics (Geometric Wavefronts)** | Approximation of propagation as rays or surfaces of constant phase; used in high-frequency limit (geometrical optics) and coverage planning. |
| **Modulation( Signal Encoding Models)** | Mathematical representation of amplitude, frequency, phase modulation; used in communication theory and signal processing. |
| **Stochastic (Noise Models)** | Statistical formalism for interference, fading, thermal noise; e.g., Rayleigh, Rician distributions; used in wireless channel modeling. |
| **Quantum Formalism (Photon Representation)** | (E = hf); used when analyzing energy transfer at single-photon scales or in quantum RF applications. |

### How many waves can propagate in the electromagnetic field simultaneously? Is there a limit?

Not in the science (field perturbation sense) - but in the pragmatic sense  (the patterns of perturbation - so called waves):

- **Theoretically (classical EM):** infinite waves can propagate simultaneously due to linear superposition.
- **Practically:** limited by frequency spectrum, available energy, medium nonlinearity, and boundary conditions.
- **Quantum perspective:** still no fundamental limit; many photons/waves can coexist, but modes and detection resolution set constraints.
