# Energy

> In this note, we explore the powerful abstraction of energy—and its related properties and phenomena—covering energy and associated concepts as treated in thermodynamics.
>

> NOTE: A VERY HARD AND SOPHISTICATED CONCEPT -  https://curtjaimungal.substack.com/p/what-is-energy-actually
>

> **Energy** is the interaction and motion of fields (at the **deepest** level of reality).
>

> **Energy is a property of matter** and is not itself matter in the traditional sense. It can be thought of as the capacity to do work or produce change. While energy itself does not have mass, it can contribute to the mass of a system due to its equivalence with mass, as described by Einstein's theory of relativity.
>

> The common denominator in all **forms of energy** is that they are all manifestations of the **ability to do work or cause change**. Energy can take many different forms, such as kinetic energy, potential energy, thermal energy, electromagnetic energy, and chemical energy, among others. However, no matter what form it takes, energy is always associated with the ability to cause changes in matter or other forms of energy.
>

> A **type of energy** is a specific form or manifestation of the capacity to do work or produce change within a physical system, such as kinetic energy, potential energy, thermal energy, or electromagnetic energy.
>

> A **thermodynamic system** is a physical or conceptual entity that is described by a set of macroscopic state variables, and its behavior is governed by the conservation of energy, the laws of entropy, and equilibrium conditions. These systems can interact with their surroundings through energy or matter exchanges, and they are classified into different types based on these interactions.

## Formulation

> What is energy?  Which are the ways to responded ‘What is energy’?
>

### Ontology

> Energy is an effective abstract — or platonic form — used to describe a property of certain systems: namely, their capacity to do work or to produce change.
>

> Energy is not a thing or substance that exists independently in reality. It is not an entity that can be isolated, located, or directly perceived. Rather, it is a **quantitative abstraction** — a conserved scalar measure that captures how configurations of matter and fields can transform, interact, and produce effects within a system.
>

> In this sense, “energy” belongs to the **realm of relational properties**, not of material substances. What we observe empirically are **processes** (motion, deformation, radiation, heat exchange); energy is the **invariant quantity** that remains conserved across such transformations.
>

> The abstraction gains ontological power not by existing *in* nature but by organizing our descriptions of it. It is a **model-theoretic construct** — an invariant across diverse domains (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, quantum theory) that reveals a deep structural unity among physical phenomena.
>

> Thus, energy is **real in its effects**, but **abstract in its being**: a *formal constraint* on how real processes can occur, not a thing among things.
>

### History

> What is the evolution of the concept of energy? Which terms were used?
>

| **Term** | **Description** | **Era** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dynamis (δύναμις)** | Greek term meaning “power” or “potential,” referring to the capacity of a thing to act or bring about change. | **Aristotle (4th c. BCE)** – distinguished *dynamis* (potential) from *energeia* (actualization). |
| **Energeia (ἐνέργεια)** | “Being-at-work”; the actuality or realization of potential. Philosophical, not yet quantitative. | **Aristotle (4th c. BCE)** – used in metaphysical and biological contexts. |
| **Vis (Force)** | Latin for “force” or “strength”; used to denote a body’s power of acting. | **Roman and Medieval Natural Philosophy** (1st–14th c.) |
| **Impetus** | Hypothetical internal force imparted to projectiles, sustaining motion after contact. | **Jean Buridan, Nicole Oresme (14th c.)** – precursor to momentum. |
| **Force vive (Living force)** | Defined as ( mv^2 ); used to describe the “living force” of a moving body. Early notion of kinetic energy. | **Leibniz (late 17th c.)** – contrasted with Descartes’s *quantity of motion* ( mv ). |
| **Quantity of Motion** | Product of mass and velocity ( mv ); conserved in collisions (in Descartes’ view). | **Descartes (17th c.)** – mechanistic philosophy. |
| **Vis mortua (Dead force)** | Potential energy before motion, e.g., compressed spring or raised weight. | **Leibniz (late 17th–early 18th c.)** |
| **Work** | The product of force and distance, measuring mechanical effort. Introduced as a quantifiable measure. | **Corliss, Smeaton, Joule (18th–19th c.)** |
| **Mechanical Equivalent of Heat** | Discovery that heat and mechanical work are equivalent forms of energy. | **James Prescott Joule (1840s)** |
| **Energy** | Unified concept encompassing all forms of capacity to do work—mechanical, thermal, electrical, etc. | **Mid–19th c.** – **William Thomson (Lord Kelvin), Rudolf Clausius** |
| **Conservation of Energy** | Principle that total energy in a closed system remains constant. | **Helmholtz (1847)** – *Über die Erhaltung der Kraft* |
| **Potential Energy / Kinetic Energy** | Formal division of energy into stored and motion-related forms. | **19th c. Mechanics & Thermodynamics** |
| **Internal Energy / Free Energy** | Thermodynamic refinements linking energy to entropy and state functions. | **Clausius, Gibbs (late 19th c.)** |
| **Mass–Energy Equivalence** | Energy and mass are interconvertible: ( E = mc^2 ). | **Einstein (1905)** |
| **Quantum Energy (Quanta)** | Energy is discrete, transferred in quanta ( E = h\nu ). | **Planck (1900), Einstein (1905)** |
| **Field Energy** | Energy stored in electromagnetic or gravitational fields. | **Maxwell, Faraday (19th–20th c.)** |
| **Vacuum / Zero-Point Energy** | Quantum fluctuations imply nonzero energy in vacuum state. | **Quantum Field Theory (20th c.)** |
| **Dark Energy** | Hypothetical energy driving the accelerated expansion of the universe. | **Late 20th–21st c. Cosmology** |

## Measure

> How to measure energy?
>

| Measurement | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Joule (J)** | SI unit of energy; measures work done or energy transferred. 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 W·s. |
| **Calorie (cal)** | Non-SI unit of energy, commonly used in food. 1 cal ≈ 4.184 J. |
| **Kilocalorie (kcal)** | 1 kcal = 1,000 cal; used for food energy. |
| **Electronvolt (eV)** | Energy gained by an electron moving through 1 volt; commonly used in atomic and particle physics. 1 eV ≈ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J. |
| **Kilowatt-hour (kWh)** | Energy used by a device of 1 kW running for 1 hour. 1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J; common in electricity billing. |
| **British Thermal Unit (BTU)** | Energy required to raise 1 lb of water by 1°F; used in heating/cooling. 1 BTU ≈ 1,055 J. |

## QA

### How to quantify the amount of energy in a system?

> Energy is a rather complex term;  with many flavors; so there is not a universal formula.
>

### Why is the field Called Thermodynamics?

> Thermodynamics literally means “the science of heat and its ability to produce work,” reflecting its historical roots in studying engines and energy conversion.
>

> In the **19th century**, scientists were trying to understand **steam engines** and how **heat could be converted into mechanical work**.
>

> Its scope has expanded far beyond heat engines to encompass **all forms of energy, work, and the fundamental limits of systems**, including chemical, electrical, and even informational processes—but the original name “thermodynamics” stuck.
>

> A more descriptive or modern name might be: **“Energy Dynamics”** or **“Energetics”**, since the field studies **energy transformations and the constraints imposed by the laws of nature**, not just heat.
>
