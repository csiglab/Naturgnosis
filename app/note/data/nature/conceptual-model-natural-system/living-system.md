# Living System

> **Living Systems:** Living systems are complex, self-organizing, and adaptive entities composed of cells that maintain homeostasis, undergo metabolism, respond to stimuli, reproduce, and evolve over time. These systems range from single-celled organisms to multicellular organisms and ecosystems, exhibiting emergent properties across hierarchical levels—molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, organism, population, and ecosystem.
>

> **Quasi-living systems** are entities that exhibit some—but not all—defining characteristics of life, such as reproduction, evolution, or response to stimuli, but lack autonomous metabolism or cellular structure. They depend on interaction with fully living organisms or environments to express these traits, and remain inert or inactive in isolation.
>

> Effective abstraction used to organized my knowledge of Biology.
>

Gray Areas:

- Viruses: They reproduce and evolve but lack independent metabolism and cellular structure.
- Prions: Infectious protein particles that cause diseases, but they are not made of cells or nucleic acids.
- Artificial Life: Simulations or synthetic biological systems created by humans blur the boundaries between living and non-living.

## Hypothesis on the Origin of Life

> **Chemical evolution**: This theory proposes that life originated from self-replicating molecules that arose through chemical change. In this scenario, simple organic molecules in the early Earth’s environment reacted with each other to form more complex molecules, such as RNA and DNA, which can self-replicate.
>

> **RNA world:** The RNA world hypothesis suggests that RNA was the first self-replicating molecule on Earth. RNA molecules can act as both genetic material and enzymes, which could have allowed for the formation of an autonomous RNA-based replication and metabolism system.
>

> **Panspermia:** The hypothesis proposes that life may have originated elsewhere and was transported to Earth on meteorites or comets. This theory suggests that extraterrestrial sources could have seeded the building blocks of life on Earth.
>

> **Hydrothermal vents:** Some researchers propose that life may have originated in hydrothermal vents, areas on the seafloor where hot water and minerals are released from the Earth’s crust. These environments may have provided a stable and nutrient-rich environment for forming self-replicating molecules.
>

## Taxonomy

> **Taxonomy** in biology is the scientific discipline concerned with the classification, naming, and organization of living organisms into hierarchical groups based on their shared characteristics, evolutionary relationships, and genetic similarities.
>

| **Taxonomy** | **Key Features** | **Tools/Methods** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linnaean Taxonomy** | Hierarchical system with fixed ranks (e.g., kingdom, phylum, class). | Morphological traits. |
| **Phylogenetic Taxonomy (Cladistics)** | Classifies organisms based on evolutionary relationships (common ancestry). | Cladograms, molecular data (DNA, RNA). |
| **Virus Taxonomy** | Classifies viruses based on genetic material, structure, and replication. | Genetic and structural analysis. |
| **Microbial Taxonomy** | Focuses on bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protists. | Genetic sequencing (e.g., 16S rRNA). |
| **Plant Taxonomy** | Classifies plants based on morphology, genetics, and ecology. | Morphological and genetic analysis. |
| **Animal Taxonomy** | Classifies animals based on anatomy, genetics, and development. | Morphological and genetic analysis. |
| **Fungal Taxonomy** | Classifies fungi based on reproductive structures and genetics. | Morphological and genetic analysis. |
| **Protist Taxonomy** | Classifies protists (eukaryotic microorganisms). | Morphological and genetic analysis. |
| **Numerical Taxonomy (Phenetics)** | Classifies organisms based on overall similarity using statistical methods. | Statistical analysis of phenotypic traits. |
| **Ecotype Taxonomy** | Classifies organisms based on ecological niches and adaptations. | Ecological and genetic analysis. |
| **Chemotaxonomy** | Classifies organisms based on chemical composition (e.g., proteins, lipids). | Biochemical analysis. |
| **Molecular Taxonomy** | Uses molecular data (DNA, RNA, proteins) to classify organisms. | DNA barcoding, whole-genome sequencing. |
| **Paleotaxonomy** | Classifies extinct organisms based on fossil evidence. | Fossil analysis. |

### **Phylogenetics Methods**

> **Phylogenetic methods** are a set of scientific techniques and computational approaches used to **infer evolutionary relationships among organisms or genes** by analyzing their genetic, morphological, or molecular data.
>

| **Method** | **Description** | **Key Features** | **Common Algorithms/Models** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Distance-Based Methods** | Calculate evolutionary distances between sequences to build a tree. | Fast and straightforward; less computationally intensive. | - UPGMA (Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean)  - Neighbor-Joining (NJ) |
| **Character-Based Methods** | Use the specific sequence data (characters) to infer phylogenies. | Accounts for the evolutionary changes of individual characters. | - Maximum Parsimony  - Maximum Likelihood |
| **Statistical Methods** | Use probabilistic models to evaluate the likelihood of different trees given the data. | More accurate, especially for large datasets, but computationally intensive. | - Bayesian Inference |
| **Cladistic Methods** | Focus on shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies) to infer relationships. | Assumes evolutionary relationships are best represented by shared derived traits. | - Maximum Parsimony |
| **Likelihood-Based Methods** | Evaluate trees based on the likelihood of observing the given data under a specific evolutionary model. | Can handle complex evolutionary models, such as variable rates of change across sites. | - Maximum Likelihood (ML) |
| **Bayesian Inference Methods** | Use a probabilistic framework to infer trees, incorporating prior knowledge and uncertainty. | Produces a distribution of probable trees rather than a single tree; requires Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. | - MrBayes, BEAST |
| **Coalescent-Based Methods** | Focus on gene genealogies and population-level processes to infer species relationships. | Incorporates population dynamics like gene flow and effective population size. | - STAR, *BEAST |
| **Genome-Wide Phylogenetics** | Uses whole-genome data for tree construction. | Provides high resolution; addresses issues like incomplete lineage sorting and horizontal gene transfer. | - ASTRAL, SNAPP |

## QA

### How do we detect micro living systems? How is the smaller unit that the human eye can see?

> **Micro-living systems** (like bacteria, protozoa, microscopic fungi, and even viruses) are **too small to see with the naked eye**, so we use **tools and techniques** to detect and study them.
>

> The **smallest unit** the human eye can typically see is about **0.1 mm** (100 µm).
>

| **Method** | **What It Detects** | **Tools Used** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Light Microscopy** | Cells, bacteria, larger organelles | Compound microscopes (1000× max) |
| **Fluorescence Microscopy** | Tagged cells or proteins | Fluorescent stains + special microscope |
| **Electron Microscopy** | Viruses, organelles, nano-structures | SEM or TEM (up to millions of ×) |
| **Culturing** | Viable microbes (bacteria, fungi) | Petri dishes with growth media |
| **Molecular Methods** | DNA/RNA (for identity & abundance) | PCR, qPCR, metagenomics |
| **Biochemical Sensors** | Enzymatic activity, ATP levels, respiration | Luminometers, biosensors |
| **Flow Cytometry** | Size, shape, and fluorescence of tiny cells | Laser-based counting system |
