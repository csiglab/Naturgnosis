# Quantum Entanglement

> Physicist John Bell later formulated Bell’s Theorem, which provided a concrete way to test the core assumptions of the EPR paradox experimentally. Bell showed that no local hidden variable theory can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics.
>

## **The EPR Paradox (1935)**

In their paper *"Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?"*, **Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR)** introduced a thought experiment suggesting that quantum mechanics might be incomplete. They argued that the theory predicts what Einstein called **“spooky action at a distance”**, implying instantaneous influence between distant particles—something that violates the principle of **locality** (i.e., no influence can travel faster than light).

### Key Points:

- Two particles interact and then separate.
- Measuring one particle appears to instantly determine the state of the other, regardless of the distance between them.
- EPR concluded that there must be **hidden variables**—unknown elements not captured by quantum mechanics—that preserve locality and realism.

## **What Is Entanglement?**

> Many people believe that entangled particles are physically connected across space.
>

**But this is not correct.**

If you have two entangled particles and perform a measurement on one, **nothing physically happens to the other**. Why? Because **interactions in quantum physics are local**.

Entanglement is not an interaction—it’s a **correlation**. Despite popular descriptions, entangled particles are not linked by some invisible force across space. They are not. They **cannot** be—because again, quantum interactions are local.

## **Measurement in Quantum Mechanics**

> A common source of confusion is the nature of measurement in quantum theory.
>

It seems intuitive that measurement should be an **interaction**—after all, detectors are made of particles. But in quantum mechanics, **measurement is not modeled as an interaction** in the traditional sense. Instead, measurement **reveals** the value of an observable.

If two particles are entangled, their observables are correlated. So, measuring one gives you information about the other. But does this **do** anything to the second particle?

Some physicists say yes; others say no. The fact that the answer is debated suggests there's **no clear evidence** that anything actually happens to the second particle upon measurement.

Einstein’s concern—his “spooky action”—was precisely this: the idea that **a measurement physically affects a distant particle**. He doubted it. And there is still **no evidence** to support that such an influence exists.

## **Clarifying Bell’s Theorem**

> Bell’s Theorem demonstrates that no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics.
>

What Bell showed is not that entanglement causes spooky action, but that the **statistics of entangled systems** can't be explained by any local hidden variable model. Entangled particles share a **common cause**—whatever physical process entangled them. That’s the source of their correlation.

Ironically, the so-called “spooky” effect arises from the **classical outcome** you get after measuring a quantum system. It's not the quantum entanglement itself that is nonlocal—**the entangled state evolves unitarily and locally**—but the **projection** onto classical outcomes that gives the appearance of nonlocality.

In other words, the “spookiness” isn't in the quantum dynamics; it's in how we interpret the outcomes.
