# Behavioral

> Surface level study of the brain outputs.
>

> **Behavioral Science** is the interdisciplinary empirical and theoretical investigation of the principles governing the **observable and inferable behaviors of agents**, primarily biological, under various internal and external constraints. It integrates methods from experimental psychology, cognitive science, economics, sociology, anthropology, and neuroscience, employing formal modeling, statistical inference, and controlled experimentation to construct predictive and explanatory frameworks.
>

> **Behavioral Science** aims to identify lawful regularities, optimize interventions, and characterize decision-making, learning, adaptation, and social interaction within and across system levels (individual, dyadic, collective).
>

> **Behavioral Science** ≈ (Empirical Measurement + Theoretical Modeling) ∘ (Agent Behavior under Constraint)
>

> **Behavior**: Observable actions or responses of an organism that reflect underlying cognitive processes such as perception, decision-making, attention, learning, and motor planning. In cognitive science, behavior is both an output of internal processes and a source of data for inferring those processes.
>

> **Behavior** is the observable output or set of responses of a system or agent over time as a function of its internal state and external inputs or environment.
>

> Note: Behavior is both (one time output) and many times (pattern like habit, etc.).
>

## Field

| Field | Description | Primary Focus | Common Methods |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive Psychology** | Studies internal mental processes | Perception, memory, reasoning, decision-making | Experiments, reaction time, modeling |
| **Behavioral Neuroscience** | Links behavior to brain structure and function | Neural substrates of behavior and cognition | Neuroimaging (fMRI, EEG), lesion studies |
| **Behavioral Economics** | Studies decision-making under real-world constraints | Biases, heuristics, utility, choice architecture | Experiments, game theory, field studies |
| **Social Psychology** | Examines how individuals are influenced by others | Norms, conformity, group behavior, identity | Lab studies, surveys, priming |
| **Developmental Psychology** | Studies behavior across the lifespan | Learning, attachment, cognitive and emotional growth | Longitudinal studies, developmental tasks |
| **Comparative Psychology / Ethology** | Studies behavior across species | Evolutionary roots, animal behavior | Field observation, conditioning, ethograms |
| **Organizational Behavior** | Examines behavior within institutions | Decision-making, motivation, communication | Case studies, organizational experiments |
| **Cultural Psychology** | Studies how culture shapes behavior and mind | Norms, values, meaning systems | Cross-cultural studies, ethnography |
| **Educational Psychology** | Applies behavioral science to learning contexts | Instruction, motivation, assessment | Controlled studies, classroom observation |
| **Health Psychology** | Studies behavior's role in health and illness | Behavior change, stress, compliance | Intervention trials, self-report, physiology |
| **Human Factors / Ergonomics** | Studies behavior in designed environments | Interaction with tools, usability, safety | Task analysis, simulations, interface testing |
| **Behavioral Data Science / Computational Behavioral Science** | Uses data and modeling to study behavior | Large-scale behavior patterns, predictions | Machine learning, agent-based models |
| **Psychometrics** | Measures psychological constructs | Intelligence, personality, values | Scale construction, factor analysis |

## Ontology (What do we observe?)

> Behavioral sciences focus on the **observation, modeling, and explanation of behavior** — the externally observable and internally inferred **patterns of action and reaction** of individuals, groups, and systems in response to stimuli, contexts, goals, or internal states.
>

### Behavioral Unit

| Entity | Description | Observables |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Individual behavior** | Actions and decisions of a single agent in a context. | Movement, speech, facial expression, choices |
| **Group behavior** | Collective dynamics, coordination, norms, and shared patterns. | Conformity, leadership, polarization, synchronization |
| **Organizational behavior** | Structured interactions within institutions. | Work roles, decision processes, communication flows |
| **Societal behavior** | Large-scale social patterns and collective phenomena. | Cultural rituals, mass movements, economic trends |

### Behavioral  Driver

| Category | Description | Example Observables |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive** | Perception, reasoning, memory, attention, emotion. | Decision latency, error rates, self-reports |
| **Affective** | Emotional states influencing behavior. | Physiological signals, facial emotion recognition |
| **Motivational** | Needs, goals, drives, reinforcement structures. | Choices under trade-offs, effort levels |
| **Social** | Influence from others, roles, norms. | Peer imitation, conformity rates, sanctions |

### Context

| Type | Description | Relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physical context** | Space, tools, sensory environment. | Modulates perception and action |
| **Social context** | Presence and behavior of others. | Alters norms, pressure, communication |
| **Cultural context** | Shared meanings, values, beliefs. | Shapes interpretative frames and scripts |
| **Institutional context** | Rules, roles, constraints, incentives. | Structures behavioral regularities |

## Epistemology (How do we observe and know?)

> In behavioral sciences, knowledge is generated through **empirical observation**, **systematic measurement**, **theoretical modeling**, and **causal inference**. The goal is to build validated representations of behavior and its determinants across levels — from individual cognition to societal patterns.
>

### Levels of Description

> …
>

| **Level** | **Description** | **Example Questions** | **Typical Methods** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Phenomenological** | Describes behavior as it is experienced or observed externally. | What behavior is observed? How does it manifest? | Behavioral observation, self-report, qualitative descriptions |
| **Functional** | Focuses on the purpose or function of behavior relative to outcomes. | What function does the behavior serve? What are its consequences? | Functional analysis, contingency analysis, reinforcement schedules |
| **Cognitive / Representational** | Explores internal mental states and processes underlying behavior. | What are the beliefs, intentions, perceptions driving behavior? | Cognitive testing, neuroimaging, computational modeling |
| **Neurobiological** | Examines brain systems and neural mechanisms that produce behavior. | What brain regions and circuits are involved? How do neurotransmitters modulate behavior? | Brain imaging (fMRI, EEG), neurophysiology, pharmacology |
| **Genetic / Evolutionary** | Investigates inherited predispositions and evolutionary origins of behavior. | How has behavior evolved? What genes influence it? | Behavioral genetics, comparative studies, molecular biology |
| **Environmental / Ecological** | Looks at the role of context and environment in shaping behavior. | How does environment influence behavior? What social or physical factors matter? | Field studies, social network analysis, ethnography |

### Observation Modality

| Modality | Description | Techniques |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct observation** | Recording overt behaviors in natural or experimental settings | Field notes, video analysis |
| **Self-report** | Access to internal states via subjective introspection | Surveys, interviews, experience sampling |
| **Physiological measures** | Infer affective or cognitive states from body signals | EEG, fMRI, skin conductance, eye-tracking |
| **Behavioral traces** | Indirect data from actions or digital footprints | Clickstreams, GPS data, social media logs |
| **Experimental manipulation** | Controlled intervention to assess causality | Randomized trials, factorial designs |

### Measurement

| Target | Measurement Technique | Validity Considerations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive processes** | Reaction time, working memory span, error patterns | Internal consistency, construct validity |
| **Affective states** | Facial recognition, heart rate variability | Cross-validation with self-reports |
| **Social behavior** | Social network analysis, group task performance | Ecological validity, interrater reliability |
| **Norms & attitudes** | Likert scales, IAT (implicit association test) | Cultural invariance, test-retest reliability |

### Meta Theory

> Which kinds of meta theories underlies psychological Models and theories>
>

| **Meta Theory** | **Description** | **Evaluation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Behaviorism** | Focuses on observable behavior and environmental contingencies; rejects introspection and mentalism. Behavior is learned through conditioning (classical and operant). | Empirically rigorous and predictive; overly reductionist, ignoring cognition and internal representation. |
| **Cognitivism** | Views the mind as an information-processing system that represents, stores, and manipulates symbols. Emphasizes perception, memory, and reasoning. | Enabled formal models of mental processes; risks oversimplifying embodiment, emotion, and context. |
| **Humanism** | Centers on subjective experience, self-actualization, and personal meaning. Emphasizes autonomy, growth, and holistic understanding of the person. | Restored the study of consciousness and values; criticized for being vague and methodologically weak. |
| **Psychoanalysis** | Explains behavior as driven by unconscious motives, conflicts, and developmental fixations. Uses interpretive rather than experimental methods. | Introduced deep models of motivation and personality; low empirical verifiability and theoretical flexibility. |
| **Neuropsychological** | Attributes psychological processes to neural, genetic, and biochemical mechanisms. Uses brain imaging and biological data. | Grounded in measurable mechanisms; reductionist when detached from psychological and social context. |
| **Evolutionary Psychology** | Explains cognition and behavior as adaptations shaped by natural selection to solve ancestral problems. | Integrates biology and psychology; prone to speculative “just-so” stories and weak empirical grounding. |
| **Sociocultural Theory** | Posits that mind and behavior are shaped by social interaction, language, and cultural tools (e.g., Vygotsky). | Captures contextual and historical embeddedness; difficult to operationalize for predictive modeling. |
| **Constructivism** | Holds that knowledge and reality are actively constructed by cognitive agents, not passively received. | Highlights subjectivity and learning dynamics; can drift toward relativism or anti-realism. |
| **Functionalism** | Focuses on the functions of mental processes in adaptation and survival rather than their structures. | Early integrative vision linking mind and environment; conceptually diffuse and methodologically outdated. |
| **Gestalt Psychology** | Emphasizes perception and cognition as organized wholes rather than aggregates of parts. | Illuminated principles of perception and problem-solving; lacked a general theory of underlying mechanisms. |
| **Ecological Psychology** | Models behavior as emerging from organism–environment systems; focuses on affordances and self-organization. | Integrative and dynamic; complex to formalize experimentally. |
| **Phenomenology** | Studies lived experience, meaning, and existence prior to theoretical abstraction. | Deep insight into human subjectivity; limited by interpretive and non-empirical methodology. |
| **Critical Psychology** | Examines power, ideology, and societal structures shaping psychological theory and practice. | Reveals hidden biases in psychological discourse; weaker on explanatory and mechanistic modeling. |
| **Dynamic Systems** | Treats psychological processes as nonlinear, time-dependent, and emergent from feedback systems. | Unifies multi-level processes and temporal change; mathematically demanding and empirically underdeveloped. |
