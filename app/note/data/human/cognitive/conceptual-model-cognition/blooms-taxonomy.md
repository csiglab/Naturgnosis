# Bloom’s Taxonomy

> NOTE: THIS TAXONOMY IS NOT BASED ON RIGOROUS SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATIONS.
>

> **Bloom’s Taxonomy** is a hierarchical classification of cognitive skills used in education to structure learning objectives, assessments, and curricula. It was originally developed by Benjamin Bloom and colleagues in 1956.
>

> This taxonomy is terminologically problematic. What is knowledge, exactly? Is knowledge the same as understanding? Is understanding simply deep knowledge? If so, this has implications for how we define and evaluate learning outcomes. Doesn’t understand implies(**Remembering,  Applying, Analyzing, Evaluating and Creating**)?
>

> Bloom’s taxonomy suffers from conceptual ambiguity, particularly in its use of key terms such as “knowledge” and “understanding.” The distinction between these concepts is neither consistently defined nor theoretically grounded. Is understanding simply a deeper form of knowledge, or is it a qualitatively distinct cognitive operation? Without clear definitions, the taxonomy risks imposing a rigid hierarchy on what may be multidimensional cognitive processes. These ambiguities challenge its utility in designing curricula and assessing learning outcomes with precision.
>

## Key Points About Bloom’s Taxonomy:

1. **Original Levels (1956):**

    The taxonomy classified cognitive skills into six levels, arranged from lower-order to higher-order thinking:

    1. **Knowledge** — Recall of facts, definitions, concepts
    2. **Comprehension** — Understanding meaning and interpretation
    3. **Application** — Using knowledge in new situations
    4. **Analysis** — Breaking down information into parts and understanding relationships
    5. **Synthesis** — Combining parts to form a new whole (creating)
    6. **Evaluation** — Judging value based on criteria (critical thinking)
2. **Revised Taxonomy (2001):**

    Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl updated Bloom’s Taxonomy to reflect modern understanding of cognition. They changed nouns to verbs to emphasize active processes:

    1. **Remembering**
    2. **Understanding**
    3. **Applying**
    4. **Analyzing**
    5. **Evaluating**
    6. **Creating**

## Common Mistakes:

- Misspelling: *“Bloom Taxonomy”* → Correct: **Bloom’s Taxonomy** (with apostrophe s)
- Treating it as a flat list instead of a hierarchical model emphasizing cognitive complexity
- Ignoring the revised taxonomy which uses verbs and reordered levels

## Is Bloom’s taxonomy a valid representation of how human cognition works?

- **Bloom’s taxonomy is not a scientifically accurate model of how the brain or cognition truly works.**
- It is **a useful heuristic framework for organizing educational objectives** but oversimplifies the complexities and fluidity of real human cognition.

Limitations as a Cognitive Model

1. **Oversimplification of cognition**
    - Human cognition is massively parallel, dynamic, and non-linear.
    - Bloom’s linear hierarchy implies a strict progression, but in reality, people often jump between levels or perform multiple processes simultaneously.
2. **Terminological ambiguity**
    - The taxonomy uses broad terms like “understanding” and “knowledge” without clear, consistent definitions.
    - Cognitive science distinguishes many types of memory, comprehension, and reasoning processes that aren’t fully captured here.
3. **Lack of neuroscientific grounding**
    - Bloom’s taxonomy predates much modern cognitive neuroscience.
    - It does not incorporate mechanisms such as working memory limits, attentional control, or emotional influences on cognition.
4. **Ignores individual differences**
    - Cognitive processes vary widely depending on experience, expertise, context, and motivation.
    - Bloom’s taxonomy treats cognition somewhat generically.
