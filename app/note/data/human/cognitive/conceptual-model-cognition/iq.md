# IQ

> An **IQ test** is a **standardized psychometric instrument** designed to estimate an individual's **latent general cognitive ability** (*g*) by measuring performance across a **battery of subtests**, each intended to probe distinct but correlated **cognitive domains** (e.g., verbal comprehension, working memory, processing speed, visual-spatial reasoning). These subtests are calibrated through **norm-referenced scaling** based on a **representative sample population**, typically using a **Gaussian distribution** with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15.
>

> IQ scores are computed via **raw-to-scaled score transformations** employing **Item Response Theory (IRT)** or **Classical Test Theory (CTT)** models. These transformations correct for item difficulty and discriminate power to yield a **standard score** reflective of an individual’s position on the **distribution of latent intelligence**.
>

> The underlying assumption of IQ testing is that cognitive ability is a **hierarchically-structured latent trait** (per **Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory** or **Spearman's g-factor model**) that manifests in measurable task performance. Validity and reliability of IQ tests are established through **factor analytic methods**, **test-retest correlations**, and **predictive validity studies**, particularly regarding educational and occupational outcomes.
>

## Methodology Criticism

> Cognitive abilities are emergent properties arising from the complex interplay of neurobiology, development, culture, motivation, and experience. Because of this complexity, reducing cognition to a single static score like IQ oversimplifies a dynamic, context-sensitive system and risks reifying the score as an inherent, fixed trait. Such reduction is epistemologically problematic and often ignores important environmental and developmental factors.
>

> However, IQ scores can still be useful tools when used carefully—not as definitive measures of innate intelligence, but as practical indicators for targeted interventions, program evaluations, or as control variables in research. They help flag individuals who may benefit from additional support or track population-level cognitive trends, provided their limitations are acknowledged.
>

> The key ethical and scientific constraint lies in how these scores are interpreted and applied. Using IQ scores to guide support or identify needs is appropriate, but claiming they represent immutable ability or making consequential policy decisions solely on them is flawed and misleading.
>

## **Norm-referenced Test**

> A **Norm-referenced test** is a type of assessment designed to compare and **rank test takers relative to one another**, rather than measuring them against a fixed standard or criterion.
>

The key features are:

- **Purpose:** To determine how an individual’s performance relates to a predefined reference group (the “norm group”).
- **Scoring:** Scores are often reported as percentiles, standard scores, or ranks, showing where a person stands compared to peers.
- **Examples:** Many IQ tests (e.g., Wechsler scales, Stanford-Binet) are norm-referenced—they assess cognitive ability by comparing a test taker’s performance to a representative sample.
- **Use:** Useful for identifying relative standing, such as determining giftedness or learning difficulties, but not for measuring mastery of specific content.

To create and administer a **norm-referenced test**, follow these general steps:

1. **Develop the Test Items**
    - Design questions or tasks that measure the construct of interest (e.g., cognitive ability, academic skill).
    - Ensure items cover a range of difficulty levels to differentiate among test takers.
2. **Select a Representative Norm Group**
    - Gather a large, diverse sample that reflects the population for whom the test is intended (age, education, region, socioeconomic status, etc.).
    - This group will serve as the baseline for comparison.
3. **Pilot the Test**
    - Administer the test to the norm group.
    - Collect item responses and analyze item performance (difficulty, discrimination).
4. **Establish Norms**
    - Calculate raw scores and convert them into standardized scores (e.g., percentiles, z-scores, T-scores) based on the norm group’s distribution.
    - Develop tables or scoring guides to interpret individual scores relative to the norm.
5. **Administer the Test to Individuals**
    - Give the test under standardized conditions.
    - Score each individual’s raw results and convert to norm-referenced scores using the established norms.
6. **Interpret and Report**
    - Use the norm-referenced scores to determine how an individual compares to the norm group (e.g., “above average,” “below average”).
    - Use results for placement, selection, or diagnostic purposes, keeping in mind the test’s limitations.

### Test Normalization

> Calculate raw scores and convert them into standardized scores (e.g., percentiles, z-scores, T-scores) based on the norm group’s distribution.
>

Step 1: Collect Raw Scores:

- Each test taker’s **raw score** is the total number of correct answers (or points) they got on the test.

Step 2: Calculate Norm Group Mean and Standard Deviation

- From the norm group data, calculate:
    - **Mean (μ)**: Average raw score of all individuals in the norm group.
    - **Standard deviation (σ)**: How many scores vary around the mean.

Step 3: Compute Z-score for an Individual

- The **z-score** tells how many standard deviations an individual’s raw score is from the norm mean.

$z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$

Where:

- $X$ = individual's raw score
- $\mu$ = mean raw score of norm group
- $\sigma$ = standard deviation of norm group scores
