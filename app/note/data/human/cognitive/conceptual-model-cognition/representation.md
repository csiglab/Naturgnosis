# Representation

> In this note, we will explore the fundamental concept of **mental representation**—an internal, structured stand-in for information such as objects, events, concepts, or states of the world—that the mind uses to think, reason, and guide behavior, **by** examining its underlying substrates, mechanisms, and functional roles.
>

> **A mental representation of reality** is the first step by which we become explicitly aware of it, gain control over it, and transform it from an **undifferentiated stream of sensory input** into a **structured, meaningful, and intelligible experience**. The second step is to become explicitly aware of the representation itself by assigning it a cognitive handle—a name that allows us to manipulate, communicate, and reason about it.
>

> 🚧 **Note:** This is a **theoretical construct**. We do not yet know the exact way the brain represents -, only that **representation is a key aspect of cognition**. Therefore, we should avoid imposing any single assumed form of representation.
>

> **Note:** Mental representation is a state of the brain that **encodes or reflects some aspect of the world**, and it should not be confused with **neural  activity - representation** itself or formal representations of that neural activity (state).
>

> Note: Some mental representations emerge during their operationalization. In working memory, they consist of multiple parts bound together by a scaffold. During the operationalization process, these parts are brought into working memory as needed.
>

> NOTE: THIS IS THE MOST RELEVANT THEORETICAL CONSTRUCT IN COGNITIVE SCIENCE.
>

> **Mental representation of reality** is the means by which we become explicitly aware of it, gain control over it, and transform it from an **undifferentiated stream of sensory input** into a **structured, meaningful,  intelligible** experience.

### Representation Level

> A **representation level** refers to a distinct degree of abstraction or structuring in a mental model, capturing how an observer internally encodes, organizes, and manipulates aspects of reality. Each level reflects a progressively richer and more actionable form of mental representation, ranging from raw sensory input to applied, context-aware judgment.
>

> **Note:** These levels are not rigid compartments; they represent **progressive depth and abstraction** in mental representation, from perception to applied judgment.
>

> See the **following effective concepts**, also organized into higher levels of mental representation.
>

> Concepts such as **data, information, knowledge, understanding, and wisdom** can serve as effective abstractions for different levels of mental representation. However, each must be carefully defined in terms of how it relates to mental representation itself.
>

| **Level** | **Description** | Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data** | Raw sensory or symbolic input; unprocessed and context-free. | A temperature reading “32°C” or the word “apple.” |
| **Idea** | Initial mental impression or thought fragment; not yet structured. | Thinking “fruit might be good for health.” |
| **Concept** | Structured abstraction; generalizes across instances; a stable mental category. | The concept of “apple” as a type of fruit. |
| **Information** | Data or concepts placed in context; meaningful relationships start to emerge. | “Apples are rich in fiber and help digestion.” |
| **Understanding** | Grasping relationships, patterns, causes, and dynamics; mental model of how things work. | Understanding why eating apples contributes to health. |
| **Knowledge** | Internalized and validated understanding; structured and actionable mental representation. | Knowing how diet affects health and how to use that knowledge in planning meals. |
| **Wisdom** | Meta-level ability to apply knowledge with judgment, foresight, and values; considers consequences and context. | Advising someone on dietary choices for long-term well-being, balancing health, taste, and cultural factors. |

### Mechanism

> How are mental representations instantiated in the brain, across both low-level and high-level structures and mechanisms? What are the mechanisms used to grow mental representations? Which are the neural substrate of Mental Representations?
>

| **Mechanism** | **Description** | **Tag(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Neural Firing Pattern** | Information is encoded in the rate, timing, and pattern of action potentials across individual neurons. | Coding, Single-Neuron |
| **Synaptic Weight** | Strength of synaptic connections encodes learned associations via plasticity mechanisms such as Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) and Long-Term Depression (LTD). | Plasticity, Memory |
| **Population Coding** | Information is distributed across the activity of many neurons, enabling robust and high-capacity representation. | Distributed, Robustness |
| **Oscillatory Synchronization** | Neurons coordinate their activity through rhythmic oscillations (gamma, theta, beta) to bind features and integrate information across networks. | Temporal Coding, Binding |
| **Neural Ensemble** | Groups of neurons that co-activate represent specific percepts, concepts, or memories, forming stable attractor states. | Attractor Dynamics, Concept Representation |
| **Sparse Coding** | Only a small subset of neurons is active at a time, improving efficiency, storage, and noise reduction. | Efficiency, Compression |
| **Hebbian Plasticity** | "Neurons that fire together, wire together" — co-activation strengthens connections, stabilizing representations. | Learning, Associative |
| **Higher-Order Integration** | Cortical pyramidal neurons and association areas integrate inputs from lower-level areas, producing abstracted and generalized representations. | Abstraction, Hierarchy |
| **Dimensionality Reduction** | Neural circuits project high-dimensional inputs into lower-dimensional manifolds (e.g., via recurrent networks), improving efficiency and generalization. | Compression, Manifold Learning |
| **Attractor Dynamics** | Recurrent neural networks settle into stable activation patterns (attractors) that correspond to concepts or memories. | Stability, Memory |
| **Synfire Chains** | Sequential firing of neuron groups encodes temporal patterns such as sequences and motor plans. | Temporal, Sequence Encoding |
| **Predictive Coding** | Hierarchical circuits encode predictions of sensory input and error signals, refining representations through feedback. | Bayesian, Inference |
| **Dendritic Computation** | Nonlinear processing in dendritic branches enables local sub-representations and context-sensitive integration. | Microcircuit, Integration |
| **Neurotransmitter Modulation** | Neuromodulators (dopamine, acetylcholine, serotonin) regulate plasticity and stability of representations. | Modulation, Learning |
| **Oscillatory Cross-Frequency Coupling** | Interactions between oscillations at different frequencies (theta-gamma coupling, etc.) organize representation across scales. | Temporal Coordination |
| **Structural Plasticity** | Growth and pruning of dendritic spines and axonal connections reshape representational capacity over time. | Development, Long-Term Learning |

### Property

> Which are the properties of Mental Representations?
>

| **Property** | **Description** | **Example(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content-bearing** | They are *about* something (intentionality) — they refer to objects, states, or concepts beyond themselves. | A mental image of an apple represents *an apple*. |
| **Format / Structure** | Representations can be symbolic (discrete, language-like), analogical (image-like, spatial), or distributed (patterns of neural activation). | A word vs. a picture vs. a neural population code. |
| **Compositionality** | Complex representations can be built from simpler ones, allowing generativity and recombination. | "Red square" = red + square. |
| **Abstractness / Generality** | They can encode not just specific instances, but abstract categories and general rules. | Concept of *dog* beyond a single animal. |
| **Flexibility / Context-dependence** | Representations can shift meaning depending on context, goals, and environment. | "Bank" = river bank vs. financial bank. |
| **Stability vs. Plasticity** | They can remain stable across time (supporting memory) yet also change and adapt (supporting learning). | Remembering your house but also updating its details. |
| **Granularity / Resolution** | They vary in level of detail, from coarse abstractions to fine-grained sensory traces. | "Tree" vs. a vivid memory of a specific oak tree. |
| **Modality-specific vs. Amodal** | Some are tied to sensory modalities, while others are abstract and modality-independent. | A sound-image of a melody vs. the abstract concept of "music." |
| **Conscious vs. Unconscious** | Some representations are explicitly accessible to awareness, others operate implicitly. | Knowing how to ride a bike (implicit) vs. recalling a fact (explicit). |
| **Dynamic / Temporal** | They can evolve in real-time, tracking changes in the world and supporting prediction. | Representing a moving car’s trajectory. |
| **Hierarchical Organization** | They are arranged in layers of increasing abstraction, from sensory features to concepts. | Visual system: edges → shapes → objects → scenes. |
| **Efficiency / Compression** | Representations reduce redundancy, encoding essential features while ignoring irrelevant detail. | Cartoon-like abstraction of a face still conveys identity. |
| **Error-prone / Fallible** | They are not perfect mirrors of reality; distortions and biases can occur. | Optical illusions, stereotypes. |
| **Neural Realizability** | They are instantiated in neural substrates — via firing patterns, connectivity, oscillations, etc. | Place cells encoding spatial location in the hippocampus. |

## Grounding

> What do we mean by the grounding of mental representations?
>

> In cognitive science, the **grounding of mental representations** refers to how abstract symbols or mental constructs acquire meaning by being **connected to the real world**, rather than floating as arbitrary symbols. In other words, a mental representation is *grounded* when it is **anchored in perception, action, or experience**, making it meaningful and useful for cognition.
>

| **Action-Based Grounding** | Connects representations to motor actions and interactions with the environment. |
| **Experience-Based Learning** | Updates representations based on feedback from experience or observation. |

## Language

> What it’s the relation of the **Human** **Language Faculty** and **Mental Representation**? Are there share mechanisms between the two? What’s the role of language in mental representations?  What is the nature of the human language faculty? How Language Enhances Cognition?
>

> See [A Guide to Language and Cognition](https://app.notion.com/p/A-Guide-to-Language-and-Cognition-1aac0f5171ec804794dbc97bc4999e9c?pvs=21), …
>

> A mental representation is not a standalone entity; rather, it is a property or instantiation of a brain state. It can take multiple forms or types, depending on the cognitive system in which it is realized. The Human Language Faculty (HLF) represents one such possible form of mental representation.
>

> The Human Language Faculty (HLF) allows the mind to **structure, manipulate, and communicate mental representations using language**, enabling abstract thought, reasoning, and complex communication.
>

> A **language-based mental representation** is a type of mental representation that is **structured in accordance with the rules and principles of a language**, such that it can be **encoded, manipulated, and potentially verbalized** using linguistic symbols. These representations typically capture abstract, compositional, and hierarchical relationships, allowing the mind to generate, interpret, and communicate complex thoughts through language.
>

> A **non-linguistic mental representation** is a mental representation that **does not rely on language for its structure or content**. It encodes information about objects, events, concepts, or states of the world in forms such as **visual images, spatial maps, sensory-motor schemas, auditory patterns, or abstract concepts**. These representations can guide thought, perception, and action, and may or may not be **verbalizable**.
>

> Note: Some non-linguistic mental representations can be verbalized, while others  or very hard or even impossible.
>

### Problem

> As of 2025 D.C - there is no definitive answer—or even comprehensive research—on the precise relationship between mental representation and language.
>

### Hypothesis

| **Hypothesis** | **Core Claim** | **Relation to HLF & MR** | **Implications** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Linguistic Determinism (Strong Sapir-Whorf)** | Language *determines* thought; people can only think within the constraints of their language. | HLF *completely shapes* mental representations; MR are fully language-bound. | Limits on cognition are dictated by linguistic structures; cross-linguistic conceptual differences are rigid. |
| **Linguistic Relativity (Weak Sapir-Whorf)** | Language *influences* thought; habitual linguistic patterns bias cognitive processes. | HLF *modulates* MR; language affects perception, categorization, and memory without fully constraining thought. | Cognitive flexibility exists; language shapes but does not rigidly define MR. |
| **Universalist View (Cognitivism)** | Thought is *independent* of language; cognitive structures are universal and language merely **encodes pre-existing MR**. | HLF is a *tool* for expressing MR, not creating it. | Cross-linguistic differences in cognition are minor; mental representations are largely universal. |
| **Neo-Whorfian** | Language influences thought in nuanced, domain-specific ways (e.g., spatial reasoning, color perception). | HLF shapes *specific cognitive processes*; MR are influenced in systematic ways by linguistic patterns. | Language highlights habitual patterns of thought; differences across languages reflect attention and conceptual framing. |
| **Embodied Cognition** | Cognitive representations are grounded in sensory-motor experience; language maps onto these embodied experiences. | HLF links symbols to perceptual and motor-based MR; language comprehension recruits sensorimotor systems. | Thought is multimodal; language scaffolds cognition but does not generate it independently. |
| **Dual Coding Theory (Paivio)** | Cognition uses two systems: verbal (language) and nonverbal (imagery). | HLF provides verbal representations; MR exist in both verbal and nonverbal forms, often interacting. | Language and thought can be independent but interact synergistically; imagery supplements linguistic encoding. |

### Impact

> How **Language Impacts Cognition**?
>

1. **Internalization**

- Already internalized **linguistic mental representations** serve as **building blocks** for representing and reasoning about other aspects of the world.
- By internalizing language, the mind can **encode experiences, concepts, and abstract relations**, allowing complex cognitive operations to be performed **mentally**, without immediate reliance on the external environment.

2. **Cognition**

- Language **enhances thought processes** by enabling **structured manipulation** of mental representations.
- Through linguistic structures, the mind can perform **planning, reasoning, abstraction, and problem-solving** more efficiently.
- Inner speech or symbolic manipulation allows for **multi-step reasoning** that would be much harder using only non-linguistic representations.

3. **Meta-Cognition**

- Language supports **thinking about one’s own thinking**, allowing reflection, evaluation, and improvement of cognitive processes.
- This enables **self-directed learning, strategic problem-solving**, and deliberate reasoning.

4. **Communication**

- Language allows the **sharing of mental representations** with others, facilitating collaboration, teaching, and cultural transmission.
- Shared language structures enable **collective reasoning** and the development of cumulative knowledge across individuals and generations.

5. **Knowledge Organization**

- Linguistic structures allow for **categorization, labeling, and hierarchical organization** of knowledge.
- Language improves **memory encoding and retrieval**, making abstract concepts more accessible and facilitating complex reasoning.

## Knowledge

> **Knowledge** is a justified, dynamically encoded representation of true or reliably inferred states of the world, stored and updated within a system such that it enables prediction, generalization, or action under constraints of uncertainty, context, and computation.
>

> Knowledge is not just about static states; it encompasses dynamics, patterns, and, more generally, the inferences that capture the behavior of the world.
>

### Condition

| **Condition** | **Description** | **Illustration** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content / Representation** | Knowledge must be encoded in some cognitive or informational structure (propositional, procedural, or embodied). | Knowing the steps of the Pythagorean theorem; having a mental map of your city. |
| **Truth / Accuracy** | The representation must correctly correspond to the world or domain; it must be veridical. | Believing that water boils at 100°C at sea level; knowing that the Earth orbits the Sun. |
| **Justification / Warrant** | The belief or representation must arise from **epistemically reliable processes**, providing normative support beyond mere belief. | Scientific experiments demonstrating drug efficacy; reasoning from axioms in mathematics. |
| **Stability / Counterfactual Robustness** | The knowledge should remain true or justified under relevant alternative scenarios; it should not be easily falsified by small changes. | Understanding that gravity pulls objects downward even if the mass or shape of the object changes slightly; a chess player knowing optimal moves regardless of minor rule variations. |
| **Practical / Functional Adequacy** | The knowledge should enable successful orientation, prediction, or action in the relevant domain. | Using knowledge of traffic rules to navigate safely; applying algorithmic knowledge to sort data efficiently. |

### Definition

| **Area** | **Definition** |
| --- | --- |
| **Epistemology** | Knowledge is *justified true belief* (or its refinements), i.e., a belief that is true and supported by adequate reasons or warrant. |
| **Cognitive Science** | Knowledge is an internal **cognitive representation** that encodes information about the world, enabling perception, reasoning, and action. |
| **Artificial Intelligenc** | Knowledge is **structured, encoded information** that can be processed, inferred, and used for decision-making or problem-solving. |
| **Information Science** | Knowledge is **actionable information** combined with experience, context, and interpretation, enabling decision-making or problem-solving. |
| **Systems Theory** | Knowledge is the **internalized model of the environment** that supports control, prediction, and adaptation in a system. |
| **Sociology** | Knowledge is **collective and socially constructed**, arising from communication, institutions, and shared practices. |
| **Pedagogy** | Knowledge is the **learned comprehension, skills, and conceptual frameworks** that enable learners to interpret and act on information. |
| **Philosophy of Science** | Knowledge is a **coherent and empirically supported system of theories and models** that explains, predicts, or structures phenomena. |

### Taxonomy

| **Type** | **Description** | **Instance(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Declarative Knowledge** | Knowledge of facts, concepts, categories, and propositions; “knowing that.” | “Paris is the capital of France,” “Dogs are mammals.” |
| **Procedural Knowledge** | Knowledge of how to perform actions and processes; “knowing how.” | Riding a bicycle, playing the piano, solving equations. |
| **Causal Knowledge** | Knowledge of cause–effect relationships, mechanisms, and dependencies. | “Fire causes smoke,” “Overheating causes engine failure.” |
| **Preconceptual Knowledge** | Intuitive, perceptual, embodied, or tacit forms of knowledge prior to explicit concepts. | Recognizing a shape before naming it, a child distinguishing animals before knowing “dog.” |
| **Conditional (Contextual) Knowledge** | Knowledge about *when* and *why* to use certain strategies, rules, or procedures. | Knowing when to apply linear vs. nonlinear models in statistics; choosing polite vs. informal speech. |
| **Metacognitive Knowledge** | Knowledge about one’s own knowledge, limitations, and strategies; self-monitoring. | Realizing you don’t understand a concept, knowing that rehearsal improves memory. |
| **Experiential (Episodic) Knowledge** | Knowledge rooted in personal experiences and specific events. | Remembering your first day at school, recalling yesterday’s meeting. |
| **Conceptual (Structural) Knowledge** | Understanding systems, frameworks, categories, and how pieces fit together. | Knowing the periodic table structure, grasping supply-and-demand interactions. |
| **Practical (Situated) Knowledge** | Context-embedded know-how shaped by environment and practice. | Navigating a specific workplace culture, street smarts. |
| **Normative (Value-based) Knowledge** | Knowledge about norms, values, or standards that guide action. | “Stealing is wrong,” “Fair play is expected in sports.” |
| **Analogical (Transfer Knowledge)** | Applying knowledge from one domain to another by analogy or mapping. | Using knowledge of hydraulics to understand electricity. |
| **Counterfactual Knowledge** | Reasoning about what could be, what would happen under different conditions. | “If gravity were weaker, planets would drift apart.” |
| **Tacit Knowledge** | Implicit, hard-to-articulate knowledge embedded in skills, habits, and practices, often learned through experience rather than explicit instruction. | Knowing how to balance on a bicycle without being able to explain it; a craftsman’s “feel” for materials; intuitive leadership judgment. |
| **Common Knowledge** | Knowledge that is shared among members of a group, and where each member knows that the others know it, often recursively (“everyone knows that everyone knows…”). This property is important in coordination, communication, and game theory. | Social conventions (e.g., driving on the right side of the road), “The meeting starts at 9 AM” when everyone knows and knows that others know, the rules of chess in a tournament. |

### Principle

| **Principle** | **Description** |

### Theory

> Which are the main theories of Knowledge?
>

A **theory of knowledge** is a systematic framework that tries to explain:

1. **What knowledge is** (its definition and nature).
2. **How knowledge is acquired** (its sources and processes).
3. **How knowledge is justified** (the criteria for saying something counts as knowledge).
4. **What the limits of knowledge are** (what can or cannot be known).

| **Theory** | **Description** | **Author(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Classical Theory (Justified True Belief, JTB)** | Knowledge = belief that is true and justified. This is the traditional starting point in Western philosophy. | Plato (*Theaetetus*), further refined by Aristotle, medieval scholastics. |
| **Foundationalism** | Knowledge rests on secure, self-evident, or indubitable foundations (like sense data or axioms). Other beliefs build upon these. | René Descartes, John Locke, early modern rationalists and empiricists. |
| **Coherentism** | A belief counts as knowledge if it coheres within a consistent and mutually supporting system of beliefs. | Hegel, Otto Neurath, Brand Blanshard, Laurence BonJour. |
| **Reliabilism** | Knowledge depends on whether the belief was formed by a reliable process (e.g., perception, memory, scientific method). | Alvin Goldman. |
| **Pragmatism** | Knowledge is defined by its practical consequences — what works in guiding action and inquiry. | Charles S. Peirce, William James, John Dewey. |
| **Contextualism** | Whether someone “knows” depends on the epistemic standards of the context (e.g., everyday vs. skeptical contexts). | Keith DeRose, David Lewis. |
| **Constructivism** | Knowledge is constructed by social, cultural, and cognitive processes rather than discovered as objective fact. | Jean Piaget, Thomas Kuhn, social constructivists. |
| **Naturalized Epistemology** | Epistemology should be continuous with science, especially psychology — studying how humans actually form beliefs. | W.V.O. Quine. |
| **Social Epistemology** | Knowledge is not just individual but also collective, shaped by institutions, testimony, and information networks. | Alvin Goldman (social turn), Helen Longino, Miranda Fricker. |
| **Skepticism** | Challenges the possibility of knowledge altogether — arguing that certainty or justification can never be achieved. | Pyrrho (ancient), Sextus Empiricus, David Hume, modern skeptics. |

## Understanding

> **Understanding** is a **higher cognitive state** in which an observer forms a **deep mental representation** of a particular aspect of reality, capturing both its **structure (ontology)** and its temporal **evolution (dynamics)**.
>

> **Understanding** is the process of reverse-engineering reality’s **latent structure (its grammar)** by observing **surface phenomena** and **recursively inferring the generative rules**, constraints, and symmetries that must have produced them. It is a lossy compression of experience into an internal model that balances predictive power with cognitive thrift, updating only when prediction error exceeds a tolerance threshold. This grammar is never fully extractable—only progressively approximated through abduction, analogy, and compression—yet every successful prediction, compression, or manipulation is evidence that we have temporarily rented a fragment of reality’s source code.
>

## Limitation

> Which are the limits of mental representation? Which are the limits of understanding? What are the limits of intelligibility?
>

> **Understanding is bounded by our biology, our tools of representation, our epistemic frameworks, and the structure of reality itself.** Yet within those limits, we continuously push the boundary outward—through science, art, technology, and philosophy—expanding what can be rendered intelligible, even while the horizon of mystery always recedes further.
>

### **Cognitive Limits (Biological and Psychological)**

- **Perceptual constraints**: Our senses sample only a narrow slice of reality (e.g., visible light, audible frequencies). Much of the universe lies outside direct perception.
- **Cognitive bandwidth**: Working memory, attention, and reasoning are finite; we can only juggle a limited amount of complexity at once.
- **Biases**: Heuristics and emotional framing skew interpretation, limiting clear understanding.

### **Conceptual Limits (Language and Representation)**

- **Language as scaffolding**: We need concepts and words to organize thought, but these abstractions can also trap us. Some experiences resist articulation (qualia, ineffable states).
- **Category limits**: Certain realities may not fit into our established categories, making them invisible or paradoxical.
- **Abstraction barriers**: Each representation filters reality; a model clarifies some aspects while obscuring others.

### **Epistemic Limits (Knowledge and Science)**

- **Gödel’s incompleteness**: In any sufficiently powerful formal system, there are true statements that cannot be proven within the system.
- **Chaos and complexity**: Deterministic systems can be unpredictable beyond certain horizons due to sensitivity to initial conditions.
- **Quantum limits**: Uncertainty principles and observer effects set irreducible boundaries on measurement.
- **Computational irreducibility** (Wolfram): For many processes, the only way to know their future state is to let them run—no shortcut exists.

### **Existential/Metaphysical Limits**

- **The self as limit**: Understanding presupposes a standpoint; we cannot step entirely outside ourselves to grasp "the whole."
- **The infinite**: Infinity, totality, or ultimate grounds (e.g., “why is there something rather than nothing?”) may remain forever beyond comprehension.
- **Transcendence**: Some traditions hold that aspects of reality (God, the Absolute, Being itself) are fundamentally beyond rational grasp.

### **Pragmatic Limits**

- **Action vs. understanding**: In many cases, we don’t need full understanding to act effectively; humans rely on heuristics, narratives, and partial models.
- **Time and resource limits**: Even if something could be understood in principle, we may lack the lifespan, computing power, or collective effort to get there.

## Evaluation

> What is an evaluation?
>

> An evaluation is a **systematic judgment** based on criteria. For mental representations, this means defining **dimensions** along which a representation can be assessed.
>

> A **mental representation evaluation model** is essentially a **framework for systematically judging the quality and function of representations** across different dimensions. It provides criteria, methods, and procedures for assessing how well a mental representation does its job.
>

> A representation is only as good as the number and quality of problems it helps us solve. To evaluate a mental representation, we must put it into practice and observe how it performs in real problem-solving contexts.
>

| **Category** | **Dimension** | **Description** | **Core Question** | **Indicator(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Efficiency | Compaction | How effectively information is encoded with minimal redundancy or size. | Is the representation compressed without losing essential content? | Memory use, redundancy, number of active elements. |
| Structure | Granularity | Supports multiple levels of detail (fine ↔ coarse). | Can the representation shift between fine and coarse levels of detail? | Resolution scaling, multiscale mapping. |
|  | Grounding | Connection of the representation to sensory, motor, or experiential bases. | Is the representation anchored in perception or embodied experience? | Sensorimotor linkages, experiential relevance. |
|  | Modularity | Organization into distinct but interrelated subcomponents. | Can the representation be decomposed into functional parts? | Module clarity, independence, recomposability. |
|  | Integration | Ability to combine multiple sub-representations into a coherent whole. | Does it integrate different parts into a unified structure? | Cross-component connectivity, holistic coherence. |
|  | Connectivity (Integration) | Degree of linkage between the representation and other abstractions. | How well does it connect outward to related knowledge? | Number/strength of links, network centrality. |
|  | Hierarchy | Organization of information into nested or ranked levels. | Is information structured in layered levels? | Depth of hierarchy, clarity of structural relations. |
| Coherence | Coherence | Internal consistency and compatibility with related representations. | Does it “hang together” without contradictions? | Logical consistency, compatibility with other models. |
| Generality | Generality | Ability to generalize across other representations while providing greater scope or flexibility. | Does the representation apply across contexts? | Transfer success, scope of applicability, abstraction span. |
| Adaptation | Plasticity | Ease of extension or modification to new contexts. | How easily can it be reshaped or extended? | Rate of adaptation, modification effort, reusability. |
| Adaptation | Adaptability (Flexibility) | Capacity to adjust accurately and efficiently to novel conditions. | How well does it adjust to change? | Error rates under change, speed of adjustment, versatility. |
| Robustness | Sensitivity | Performance stability across perturbations or contextual shifts. | Does it remain useful under variation or noise? | Error tolerance, resilience, stability. |
| Abstraction | Abstraction | Reduction of detail, focusing on essential features/patterns. | Does it capture the “essence” while discarding irrelevant details? | Feature compression, level of generalization. |
| Accuracy | Accuracy | Closeness to the true or intended target. | Does it match the real referent or domain? | Error rates, empirical match, validation checks. |
|  | Precision | Level of fine detail in the representation. | How detailed and exact is it? | Resolution, specificity, measurement granularity. |
| Utility | Usability | Ease of access, interpretation, and application. | Can it be readily used by a cognitive system/agent? | Processing time, clarity, accessibility. |
| Awareness | Conscious Accessibility | Availability to awareness and reflection. | Can the system consciously access/manipulate it? | Reportability, introspection success, metacognitive access. |
| Cognitive Fit | Brain Capacity Affinity | Alignment with brain’s natural strengths and biases. | Does it fit naturally with cognitive architecture? | Learning speed, neural plausibility, innate bias alignment. |
| Communication | Communicability | Ease of externalization and sharing. | Can it be explained, expressed, or taught? | Symbolic expressiveness, clarity of articulation. |

## Theory Space

> What are the main theories of brain representations?
>

> **Note:** These theories are generally broad theories of cognition, not solely focused on representation. For more details, see [Cognitive](note.html?n=human/cognitive.md).
>

| **Theory** | **Representation Type** | **Description** | **History** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Symbolic (Classical) Theory** | Explicit, discrete, compositional | Minds represent knowledge using discrete symbols manipulated by rules (like language or logic) | Roots in ancient logic (Aristotle); formalized in early analytic philosophy and logic (Frege, Russell); dominant in early cognitive science and AI from the 1950s–1970s (Newell & Simon, Fodor). |
| **Connectionist (Subsymbolic) Theory** | Distributed, graded, implicit | Knowledge is encoded in distributed patterns of activation across neural-like networks | Roots in neural network research from the 1940s (McCulloch & Pitts, 1943), popularized in 1980s with Parallel Distributed Processing (Rumelhart & McClelland) |
| **Mental Imagery Theory** | Analog, depictive, quasi-perceptual | Mental representations are analog-like images resembling perception | Philosophical roots in 19th century (e.g., Francis Galton’s imagery studies), formalized in cognitive psychology from 1970s (Stephen Kosslyn) |
| **Dual-Coding Theory** | Hybrid: symbolic + depictive | Proposes two representational systems: verbal (symbolic) and nonverbal (imagistic) | Developed by Allan Paivio in late 1960s–70s, emphasizing complementary verbal and visual coding |
| **Embodied Cognition** | Sensorimotor, grounded, action-oriented | Representations grounded in sensory-motor systems and bodily interactions with environment | Origins in phenomenology and 20th-century philosophy; formalized in cognitive science from 1990s onward (e.g., Lakoff & Johnson, Varela) |
| **Conceptual Spaces Theory** | Geometric, continuous, metric | Knowledge represented geometrically as points and regions in multidimensional spaces | Proposed by Peter Gärdenfors in 2000s, building on geometric and metric theories of concept representation |
| **Dynamic Systems Theory** | Dynamical, state-space trajectories, non-symbolic | Representations are emergent patterns from dynamic interactions over time | Rooted in mathematical and physical theories from early 20th century; applied to cognition in 1980s–90s (Thelen, Smith) |
| **Prototype Theory** | Summary-based, central-tendency, graded | Concepts represented by an idealized or average member of a category | Introduced by Eleanor Rosch in the 1970s, emphasizing family resemblance and graded category membership |
| **Exemplar Theory** | Instance-based, memory-based, similarity-driven | Concepts represented by storing multiple specific instances (exemplars) | Emerged as alternative to prototype theory in 1980s–90s, with work by Medin and Schwanenflugel |
| **Fodor’s Informational (Causal) Theory** | Symbolic, atomistic, referential | Mental symbols get their meaning via lawful causal relations with properties in the world; misrepresentation explained by asymmetric dependence | Developed by Jerry Fodor (1980s–90s), especially *Psychosemantics* |
| **Inferential Role Semantics (IRS)** | Relational, inferential, role-defined | Meaning of a representation is determined by its role in inference rather than reference | Developed in philosophy of language/mind (Sellars, Harman, Block) |
| **Teleosemantics** | Functional, historical, selection-based | Representational content grounded in biological function shaped by natural selection | Developed in 1980s–90s (Millikan, Papineau, Neander) |
| **Predictive Processing** | Probabilistic, hierarchical, generative | Representations are probabilistic generative models minimizing prediction error | Roots in Helmholtz; modern form in 2000s–2010s (Friston, Clark) |
| **Enactivism** | Action-dependent, relational, minimally representational | Cognition arises from active engagement with environment; rejects internal representations or minimizes them | Radicalized embodied cognition (Varela, Thompson, Noë) |
| **Radical Anti-Representationalism** | Non-representational, dynamical, ecological | Denies that cognition requires representations at all | Philosophical stance (Gibson, Chemero, some enactivists) |
| **Structural Representation Theory** | Structural, relational, isomorphic/homomorphic | Representations preserve relational structure of what they represent (isomorphism, homomorphism) | Developed in philosophy of science and cognition (Cummins, Shea) |

>

Let’s start with a case  (**understanding probability**), two form of **mental representations**:

- [Experiment -> Sample Space -> Probability Law]: We have an **experiment** (its dynamics), a **sample space** (the set of **possible outcomes**), and a probability law that specifies how likely it is for the experiment’s outcome to be a particular element in the sample space.
- Experiment -> State Space -> Probability Law]: We have an **experiment** (its dynamics), a **state space** (the set of **possible outcomes**), and a probability law that specifies how likely it is for the experiment’s outcome to be a particular element in the sample space.
- [System -> State Space -> Probability Law]:  We have a system with its dynamics and a state space $X$. The key question then becomes: how likely is it that the next state of the system is a specific point $x$ or lies within a particular region of $X$?

Comparing Representations:

| **Criterion** | **Experiment -> Sample Space -> Probability Law** | **Experiment -> State Space -> Probability Law** | **System -> State Space -> Probability Law** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Compaction** | **Medium**. Encodes a specific process (experiment) and its outcomes (sample space). | **Medium-High**. The term "state space" can be more general than "sample space," leading to a slightly more compact representation. | **High**. Replaces "experiment" with the broader concept of a "system" with dynamics, making it highly compact and applicable to a wider range of phenomena. |
| **Fluidity** | **Medium**. Can be applied to various experiments, but the link between the experiment and its sample space is often rigid. | **High**. The use of "state space" allows for more flexible connections and a natural progression to different levels of detail (e.g., microstates within a macrostate). | **Very High**. By focusing on the "system" and "state space," this representation easily connects to other system-based concepts (e.g., physics, economics) and supports dynamic, time-evolving processes. |
| **Generality** | **Low-Medium**. Primarily applicable to traditional probability experiments (e.g., coin flips, dice rolls). | **Medium-High**. The term "state space" extends the model to more complex scenarios, like the states of a physical system or a set of possible outcomes in a game. | **Very High**. The **system** abstraction is highly general, encompassing experiments, physical systems, computational processes, and even biological phenomena. |
| **Plasticity** | **Medium**. Can be extended, but often requires significant modification if the underlying "experiment" changes significantly. | **High**. The state space framework is readily adaptable to new contexts by simply redefining the states and the probability law. | **Very High**. The core concepts of a system, its dynamics, and state space are extremely malleable and can be applied with minimal modification to new domains. |
| **Adaptability** | **Medium**. Well-suited for discrete, well-defined experiments but less adaptable to continuous or dynamic contexts. | **High**. The state space concept is inherently more adaptable, accommodating both discrete and continuous state variables and changing conditions. | **Very High**. Designed to handle novel and changing contexts by focusing on the underlying system dynamics rather than a static "experiment." |
| **Sensitivity** | **High**. Robust for its intended purpose (well-defined experiments), but breaks down if the context or dynamics change significantly. | **Medium-High**. More robust than the experiment-sample space model, as it can handle perturbations by updating the state space or probability law. | **Very High**. The focus on system dynamics makes this representation highly robust, as it can account for changes or perturbations as part of the system's evolution. |
| **Abstraction** | **Low**. The concepts are closely tied to a concrete "experiment" with tangible outcomes. | **Medium**. "State space" is a step towards greater abstraction, moving away from the physical experiment itself. | **High**. The "system" abstraction is highly abstract, simplifying details to focus on fundamental dynamics and the probabilities of state transitions. |
| **Hierarchy** | **Low**. The components are generally on a single level of abstraction. | **Medium-High**. States can be organized into hierarchies (e.g., macrostates composed of microstates). | **Very High**. Naturally supports hierarchical organization, where a "system" can contain subsystems, each with its own state space and dynamics. |
| **Accuracy** | **High**. Very accurate for its specific domain of application (well-defined experiments). | **High**. Accurate, as it provides a precise mathematical framework for describing outcomes and probabilities. | **High**. Provides a highly accurate framework for a broad range of systems, from simple experiments to complex, evolving phenomena. |
| **Precision** | **High**. Can be very precise, especially for discrete sample spaces. | **High**. Allows for high precision, accommodating both discrete and continuous variables. | **High**. The mathematical rigor of state space and probability laws ensures high precision. |
| **Usability** | **High**. Intuitive for basic probability problems and widely used in introductory education. | **Medium-High**. Requires a slightly higher level of conceptual understanding but is highly usable for a broader range of applications. | **Medium**. While powerful, it requires a more abstract and advanced cognitive framework, making it less immediately accessible for beginners. |
| **Brain Capacity Affinity** | **Low**. Using Venn diagrams(algrebraic - symbolic) is harder than seeing a space distribution of states | **Medium-High**. If the state-space is viewed in geometrical terms. | **High**. If the state-space is viewed in geometrical terms. |

### **How can the learner observe - analyze and improve it’s own mental representation of a RepTarget?**

> **Mental representations** co-evolve with practice. A strong mental representation can only be refined and enhanced through deep practice, while practice without a coherent prior mental representation will likely lead nowhere. To improve, you need to practice the right thing within the right mental framework. See  [Practice](note.html?n=human/cognitive/conceptual-model-cognition/practice.md).
>

This is an exercise in meta-cognition: becoming aware of and reflecting on your own thought processes and mental representations.

- **Externalize it** — express your mental model by describing it in words, drawing diagrams, or mapping its components.
- **Interrogate it** — probe your representation by asking questions related to sensory details, emotions, or contextual factors.
- **Compare it** — contrast your current understanding with others’ perspectives or with your previous knowledge.
- **Test it** — evaluate your representation through making predictions or drawing analogies to see how well it holds up.

### In which domains does the brain naturally excel, and in which does it struggle—especially when it comes to different kinds of computation and mental representations?

> 📝  The brain is an organ with natural strengths, but it can also develop effective representations in domains where it is not inherently optimized—for example, chess, which requires  **symbolic, and strategic reasoning** that is not the brain’s default mode. Sometimes, creating new mental representations involves **building on existing ones**, even if they don’t perfectly align with the brain’s natural capacities, rather than relying solely on the brain’s **default strengths**.
>

> **Mental representations** are internal cognitive structures that the brain constructs to model, interpret, and manipulate information from the world. These representations are closely tied to neural activity and brain architecture—they emerge from how networks of neurons encode, store, and retrieve information.
>

| **Aspect** | **Description** | **Evaluation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pattern Recognition** | Detecting regularities and patterns in sensory input (visual, auditory, etc.) | **Excellent** |
| **Visual Processing** | Rapid and detailed interpretation of complex visual scenes | **Excellent** |
| **Spatial Reasoning** | Mental manipulation and understanding of objects in space | **Excellent** |
| **Parallel Processing** | Simultaneous processing of multiple information streams | **Excellent** |
| **Associative Memory** | Linking concepts through associations for flexible recall | **Excellent** |
| **Hierarchical Structuring** | Organizing information into nested or layered categories | **Good** |
| **Emotional Processing** | Interpretation of emotions and social cues | **Excellent** |
| **Symbolic Reasoning** | Manipulation of abstract symbols, formal logic, and language | **Moderate to Weak** |
| **Sequential Processing** | Handling strictly ordered, step-by-step operations | **Moderate to Weak** |
| **Exact Numeric Computation** | Performing precise arithmetic or numeric operations | **Weak** |
| **Rule-Based Logical Processing** | Applying formal rules and algorithms | **Moderate to Weak** |
| **Language Processing** | Understanding and generating language, both spoken and written | **Good** |
| **Motor Planning** | Planning and executing complex motor sequences | **Good** |

### **What are the guidelines to develop brain-optimized mental representations?**

Meta Principles:

| **Principle** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Prioritize Meaning over Rote Information** | Focus on deep understanding and conceptual clarity rather than memorization. |
| **Support Active Engagement** | Encourage learners to interact with and manipulate the representation. |
| **Balance Novelty and Familiarity** | Introduce enough novelty to maintain attention without overwhelming. |
| **Create Multiple Retrieval Pathways** | Design for recall through varied cues and contexts. |
| **Align with Natural Cognitive Processes** | Match representation formats to the brain’s inherent strengths and biases. |

Guidelines:

| **Guideline** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Visual-spatial formats** | Use diagrams, mind maps, and infographics to leverage the brain’s strong visual processing abilities. |
| **Hierarchical organization** | Arrange information in nested categories that reflect the brain’s natural organizational structure. |
| **Pattern highlighting** | Make underlying patterns explicit using visual cues such as color, shape, or layout. |
| **Narrative integration** | Embed information within stories to enhance comprehension and recall. |
| **Metaphorical mapping** | Link abstract concepts to familiar, concrete experiences through analogy. |
| **Multisensory engagement** | Use materials that stimulate multiple senses at the same time. |
| **Strategic chunking** | Group information into meaningful units to fit within working memory limits. |
| **Interactive elements** | Include manipulable components that promote active involvement. |
| **Emotional relevance** | Tie content to personally meaningful or emotionally engaging material. |
| **Spatial metaphors** | Use physical space to map abstract concepts (e.g., timelines, conceptual maps). |
| **Embodied design** | Relate abstract ideas to bodily sensations or movements. |
| **Associative networks** | Make explicit the links between related concepts. |
| **Progressive disclosure** | Present information gradually to avoid overload. |
| **Dual coding** | Combine text and visuals to engage both verbal and visual systems. |
| **Contextual richness** | Place content in meaningful contexts to enable multiple retrieval cues. |

Pitfalls:

| **Pitfall** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Overloading working memory** | Presenting too much information at once, exceeding processing limits. |
| **Excessive abstraction** | Removing too many concrete anchors, making concepts hard to grasp. |
| **Empty (Decorative) Abstarctions** | Purely decorative elements; placeholder or routes to others abstarctions. |
| **Poor visual design** | Using cluttered, low-contrast, or inconsistent visuals that hinder comprehension. |
| **Lack of emotional connection** | Failing to make the content relevant or engaging to the learner. |
| **Ignoring retrieval cues** | Omitting context or associations that aid memory recall. |
| **Unbalanced novelty** | Either too familiar (boring) or too novel (confusing), disrupting learning. |
| **Misaligned format** | Choosing representation styles that clash with the brain’s natural processing strengths. |

### What role do these representations play in cognition?

> We gain **conceptual control over reality** through **mental representation**; awareness and naming (tags) allow us to explicitly access and manipulate these representations. That which is not represented—either implicitly or explicitly—remains an undifferentiated stream of sensory input and does not enter conscious cognition.
>

> It is entirely possible to live without a conceptual representation of “living,” and without being consciously aware of that representation by naming it. However, without such representation—and the ability to reflect on it—we cannot analyze, compare, or imagine alternative modes of living.
>

> See more in [A Guide to Language and Cognition](https://app.notion.com/p/A-Guide-to-Language-and-Cognition-1aac0f5171ec804794dbc97bc4999e9c?pvs=21).
>

### How do we define the types of mental representations?

> Types are defined by what is represented, how it is represented, and how it functions in cognition.
>

> Types of mental representations can be defined according to the **target of the representation**—what the representation refers to—as well as other dimensions such as:
>
1. **Ontological Target** – The kind of entity or phenomenon represented (object, process, property, relation, concept, state, etc.).
2. **Cognitive Form** – How the knowledge is structured or encoded (declarative, procedural, episodic, semantic, symbolic, pre-conceptual).
3. **Granularity / Level of Abstraction** – From atomic facts to complex theories or meta-representations.
4. **Functional Role** – The purpose or use of the representation (categorization, prediction, reasoning, communication, planning, skill execution).

### **Why evaluate mental representations?**

> *We evaluate to ensure that our mental models guide perception, reasoning, and action effectively.*
>

> Because representations are the **interface between reality and cognition/action.**
>
- **Accuracy:** If a representation is systematically wrong, actions based on it will fail.
- **Efficiency:** Some representations may be redundant, overly complex, or costly to maintain.
- **Generalizability:** A good representation supports reasoning in new or slightly different contexts.
- **Alignment:** Representations need to align with goals, cultural schemas, or group cognition.

### What is intelligibility?

> Intelligibility is the property of a perception, representation, concept, or piece of information that allows it to be **understood, recognized, and manipulated** by a cognitive agent. Something intelligible is not merely present; it is **structured and interpretable**, enabling reasoning, prediction, or action.
>

> Something intelligible is **not merely observed**, but **understood in a way that supports reasoning, manipulation, and forecasting**.
>

**Intelligibility** is the property of a phenomenon or representation that allows a cognitive agent to:

1. **Grasp its structure:** Identify its components, relations, and patterns.
2. **Predict its dynamics:** Anticipate how it evolves or responds under various conditions.

### What is meaning?

> Meaning arises *through representation*, and it deepens as representations become better aligned with structure and dynamics.
>

Meaning as function, not essence:

- Meaning isn’t an intrinsic property of the thing itself; it arises in the **relation between representation and cognitive use**.
- A “good representation” gives rise to meaning because it allows the agent to **orient action, make predictions, and integrate with broader knowledge**.
