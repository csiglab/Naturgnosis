## Definition

> Exactly — defining a **symbol** in a fully general sense is tricky because its meaning depends on **function, form, and context**, which vary across disciplines. But we can try to extract a **general, cross-disciplinary conception**.
>

> A **symbol** is an entity (sign, token, or representation) that **stands for or represents something else**—an object, concept, state, or relation—and is **interpreted or manipulated according to a system of rules, conventions, or functions**. Its meaning arises not from inherent properties alone but from its **relation to what it represents, the context in which it is used, and the rules that govern its interpretation or operation**.
>

> A **subsymbol** is a representation that encodes information in a **distributed, often non-discrete form**, which contributes to higher-level symbolic processing but **does not function as a fully interpretable or standalone symbol**. Subsymbols capture **patterns, features, or constraints** rather than explicit meanings.
>

> A **subsymbol** is like the “building block” of cognition: it’s **not a symbol itself**, but many subsymbols combine to form symbols and enable higher-level cognitive processes. This distinction is central in debates between **classical symbolic AI** and **connectionist / neural network approaches**.
>

## Grounding

> In cognitive science, the **grounding of mental representations** refers to how abstract symbols or mental constructs acquire meaning by being **connected to the real world**, rather than floating as arbitrary symbols. In other words, a mental representation is *grounded* when it is **anchored in perception, action, or experience**, making it meaningful and useful for cognition.
>

> Let’s imagine you go to the dictionary to look up a word, say **x**. You read the definition, but you can only truly understand it if you know the meanings of the words used in that definition. If you don’t, you have to consult other resources. At some point, you need to stop—there are **primitive words** that you were never explicitly taught but somehow just “pick up.” These primitives form the foundation that enables you to **build towers of definitions** and gradually acquire more complex knowledge - and this is the **symbol grounding problem**.
>

Theory:

- **External grounding perspective**:  Their meaning arises from connections to extra-mental elements such as sensory experiences, physical objects, or environmental interactions.
- **Internal (Chomskyan) grounding perspective:** Symbols derive meaning **internally** via **recursive self-reference** and **bootstrapping**, independent of direct links to the external world, though external factors may **still influence them but not cause by them**.
