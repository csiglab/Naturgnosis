# Reference

> In **cognitive science**, *reference* generally refers to the mental mechanism or process by which symbols, words, or representations relate to the objects, concepts, or states they stand for. It plays a crucial role in **language, perception, memory, and reasoning**.
>

> The **theory of reference** in philosophy addresses how **words, names, or symbols** relate to the **things they stand for**—how language "hooks onto" the world.
>

## Index

## Taxonomy

1. **Linguistic Reference**

- Focuses on how **words and phrases refer** to things in the world.
- Example: In the sentence *"The cat is on the mat"*, the noun phrase *"the cat"* refers to a particular animal.
- Related to **semantics** (meaning) and **pragmatics** (contextual use).
- Key theories: **Frege’s sense and reference**, **Kripke’s causal theory**, **Relevance theory** in pragmatics.

2. **Mental Representation**

- In **cognitive psychology**, reference is about how **mental representations** (like concepts or mental images) relate to actual or possible entities.
- Theories like **symbolic AI** and **connectionism** differ in how reference is handled internally.
- Example: A mental concept of *dog* refers to real-world dogs, even if abstracted.

3. **Reference in Perception**

- Deals with how perceptual systems identify and maintain contact with objects.
- Example: **Visual tracking** of a moving object maintains reference to that object over time.
- Related concepts: **Object permanence**, **binding problem**, **attentional indexing**.

4. **Demonstrative and Deictic Reference**

- Reference based on **context or pointing**, e.g., “this”, “that”, “here”, “now”.
- Important in **infant development**, **language acquisition**, and **joint attention** studies.

5. **Reference in Memory**

- Involves linking cues or indexes to **experiences** or **stored knowledge**.
- Episodic memory refers to specific past events, while semantic memory refers to general knowledge

6. **Neural and Computational Models**

- Some models try to formalize reference in terms of **neural coding**, **pointer-like mechanisms**, or **attention maps**.
- In **computational cognitive architectures**, reference is implemented via data structures linking symbols to perceptual or memory contents.

| **Referential / Causal-Historical (Kripke, Putnam)** | “Reference is fixed by an initial baptism and sustained by causal chains.” | Proper names as rigid designators; natural-kind terms; causal-historical chains | Explains how names succeed in counter-factual scenarios (Twin Earth); supports scientific realism | Qua-problem; reference change; empty names (e.g., “Vulcan”) | Kripke 1980; Putnam 1975 |
| **Descriptivism (Frege, Searle, Lewis)** | “Reference = satisfying a cluster of definite descriptions.” | Sense (Sinn), intension, clusters, analytic definitions | Handles cognitive significance (Hesperus/Phosphorus) and Frege’s puzzle | Modal rigidity failures; ignorance & error (semantic deference) | Frege 1892; Lewis 1970 |
| **Two-Dimensional Semantics (2DS)** | “There are two semantic dimensions: primary vs. secondary intensions.” | Diagonal vs. horizontal propositions; epistemic vs. metaphysical possibility | Unifies descriptivist epistemic roles with Kripkean metaphysics | Complexity; 2D matrices hard to compute | Chalmers 1996; Jackson 1998 |
| **Verificationist / Inferential Role (Dummett, Brandom)** | “Meaning is use in a practice of justification.” | Assertion conditions, inferential norms, score-keeping | Links semantics with epistemic access; anti-skeptical | Threat of semantic holism; “use” is under-specified | Dummett 1991; Brandom 1994 |
| **Prototype / Exemplar Theory (Rosch, Smith)** | “Categories refer via similarity to stored prototypes or exemplars.” | Graded structure, family resemblances, similarity metrics | Explains typicality effects, fast categorization | Fails on “no prototype” concepts; similarity metric circularity | Rosch 1978; Smith & Medin 1981 |
| **Theory-Theory (Carey, Gopnik, Keil)** | “Concepts refer via their place in an intuitive theory (mental ‘Folk’ or scientific).” | Naïve theories, causal laws, explanatory coherence | Accounts for conceptual change & child development | Holism; reference incommensurability across theories | Carey 1985; Gopnik & Meltzoff 1997 |
| **Causal-Mapping (Teleosemantics: Millikan, Neander)** | “A state refers to what it is biologically supposed to covary with.” | Proper functions, selection history, Normal conditions | Naturalizes intentionality; handles misrepresentation | Swampman; indeterminacy of functions | Millikan 1984; Neander 2012 |
| **Information-Theoretic Teleosemantics (Dretske)** | “Mental state M means F iff M has the function to carry information that F.” | Channel conditions, nomic dependence, learning period | Clear mathematical grounding in communication theory | Disjunction problem; under-determination of content | Dretske 1981, 1988 |
| **Consumer Teleosemantics (Papineau)** | “Content is fixed by what the *consumer* systems use the representation for.” | Producer–consumer distinction, decision theory | Solves indeterminacy left by pure causal accounts | Requires detailed ecological specification | Papineau 1993 |
| **Structural Representation / S-representation (Cummins, O’Brien & Opie)** | “A system S represents T iff there is an isomorphism exploitable by S to guide behavior.” | Structural isomorphism, second-order similarity | Relates directly to computational neuroscience (e.g., grid cells) | Triviality threat; how much structure is enough? | Cummins 1996; O’Brien & Opie 2004 |
| **Dynamical Systems / Enactivist (Chemero, Hutto & Myin)** | “Reference is enacted in sensorimotor loops; content is *not* reified.” | Coupled agent-environment dynamics, affordances | Avoids symbol-grounding problem; fits robotics & ecological psychology | Hard to explain offline thought or novel reference | Chemero 2009; Hutto & Myin 2013 |
| **Simulation / Embodied-Cognition (Barsalou, Glenberg)** | “Concepts *are* re-enactments of perceptual/motor states.” | Perceptual symbols, mirror neuron reuse | Empirical support from neuroimaging; explains abstract metaphor | Needs story for amodal content & false beliefs | Barsalou 1999; Glenberg 2010 |
| **Predictive-Processing / Bayesian (Friston, Clark)** | “Internal models refer by minimizing prediction error.” | Hierarchical generative models, precision-weighted prediction errors | Unifies perception, action, and learning; computational foothold | Computational intractability; “dark room” problem | Friston 2010; Clark 2016 |
| **Mental Files (Recanati)** | “Reference is routed through dynamically updated *mental dossiers*.” | Mental files, acquaintance relations, file dynamics | Integrates cognitive psychology with semantics (memory, attention) | Needs account of file individuation | Recanati 2012 |

## Name

> How names get’s there meaning?
>

> The way we name things is not necessarily related to how those names are used. This is why the intermediary notion of **reference** is important. Take, for example, a *river*: some places are still called rivers even though they no longer contain any water—there was water there once, and the name persisted.
>

> **Names contain only reference.** So, in order to know what a name means, you need to uncover its referent—because the same name can be used to refer to many different things.
>

> **Names ≠ Senses**: Names carry no inherent descriptive content—only **reference**.
>

> **Meaning = Reference resolution**.
>

Let:

- $N$ be the set of all **names**.
- $R$ be the set of all **referents** (entities in the world).
- $\mathcal{R}: N \to R$ be a **reference function** that maps each name $n \in N$ to a **set of possible referents** $\mathcal{R}(n) \subseteq R$.

**Then:**

A name $n$ has no meaning beyond its reference, i.e., $\text{Meaning}(n) := \mathcal{R}(n)$

And because: $|\mathcal{R}(n)| \geq 1 \quad \text{and possibly} \quad |\mathcal{R}(n)| > 1,$ to understand $n$, one must disambiguate it by identifying the intended referent $r^* \in \mathcal{R}(n)$.
