# Noam Chomsky


> A language is, according to Chomsky, a state obtained by a specific mental computational system that develops naturally and whose exact parameters are set by the linguistic environment that the individual is exposed to as a child. This definition, which is at odds with the common notion of a language as a public system of verbal signals shared by a group of speakers, has important implications for the nature of the mind.
> 

> **Note:** The constant thread in Chomsky’s work is a single question: *What is the nature of the language faculty as a neural-biological system, and how can it be explained? His proposal has changed, etc.*
> 

## Language

> **Language** is a species-specific, biologically endowed computational system (I-language) that generates structured pairings of sound and meaning via Universal Grammar, emerging in the mind/brain and acquired through parameter-setting from limited input.
> 

## Grammar

For Chomsky, a **grammar** is not “grammar” in the everyday sense of prescriptive rules, but a **formal, explicit theory of a language**.

Technically, in his framework:

- **Grammar** = the **internalized cognitive system** (I-language) in a speaker’s mind/brain that generates all and only the well-formed sentences of that language.
- It is a **generative system**: a finite set of rules or principles that can produce (generate) an infinite set of possible sentences, each paired with its sound (phonological form) and meaning (logical form).
- A grammar, as a scientific object, is a **model** of this mental system — specified in formal terms — that captures a speaker’s **linguistic competence**.
- In the **Principles & Parameters** and **Minimalist** periods, “grammar” refers to the *instantiated version* of **Universal Grammar** after its parameters have been set by exposure to a particular language.

## Universal Grammar

> **Note:** The idea of Universal Grammar is essentially this: we observe a phenomenon (observable speech) and ask, “What is happening under the hood? What precedes it?” Whatever that underlying mechanism is, we give it a name—*Universal Grammar*. This is not so much a formal theory as it is a revolutionary truism, one that points the field toward a particular research direction: becoming more cognitive and biologically oriented.
> 

> **Note:** **Chomsky** has proposed several models of Universal Grammar, but Universal Grammar itself is not a theory. Therefore, while individual models can be falsified or revised, Universal Grammar as a concept remains unaffected.
> 

> **Universal Grammar → Individual Grammar (I-language) → Observable speech (E-language).**
> 

> Grammar = UG (innate principles) + Parameter settings (from linguistic experience).
> 

> **Universal Grammar** is the *innate, species-specific cognitive system* that defines the space of possible human languages, consisting of a set of **principles** (fixed, universal constraints on grammar) and **parameters** (limited options that account for cross-linguistic variation). It is part of the human biological endowment, emerges early in development, and enables children to acquire complex grammatical systems rapidly despite limited and imperfect input (the *Poverty of the Stimulus* argument). UG serves as the initial state of the language faculty, which interacts with experience to yield a particular language (the *steady state*).
> 

> **Chomsky** like to use the analogy of Language Growth, not learn because not one is teaching the children.
> 

> The **UG** This hypothesis is proposed;  because the Childrens learn so much; without training, even very complex concepts.
> 

## Research Agenda

| **Period** | **Research Program** | **Core Focus** | **Key Works** | **Central Ideas** | **Impact / Legacy** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1950s–1960s** | **Generative Grammar** | Formal description of syntax as an explicit generative system | *Syntactic Structures* (1957), *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) | Language is generated by an internal grammar; competence vs. performance; syntax has deep and surface structures | Revolutionized linguistics by introducing formal rules and computational modeling of language |
| **1960s–1970s** | **Standard Theory → Extended Standard Theory** | Refinement of transformations; interface between syntax and semantics | *Aspects* (1965), *Studies on Semantics in Generative Grammar* (1972) | Transformational rules link deep structure (semantic interpretation) and surface structure (phonetic form) | Integrated syntax with semantics; set the stage for more modular theories |
| **1970s** | **Extended Standard Theory & Trace Theory** | Greater role for constraints on transformations | *Conditions on Transformations* (1973) | Constraints (e.g., island conditions) limit transformations; traces represent moved elements | Opened path for principles-based grammar rather than rule proliferation |
| **1980–1990s** | **Government and Binding (GB) Theory** | Unified syntactic theory via modules | *Lectures on Government and Binding* (1981) | Modules: X-bar theory, Case theory, Binding theory, Bounding theory, Government, Control; Principles & Parameters framework | Replaced rule-based grammar with universal principles and language-specific parameters |
| **1990s–Present** | **Minimalist Program** | Reduction of linguistic theory to optimal design | *The Minimalist Program* (1995) | Language faculty is an optimal solution given interface constraints; economy principles; derivations driven by feature checking | Simplified architecture, linking linguistics with cognitive science and biology |
| **1960s–Present** | **Biolinguistics** | Language as a biological capacity | *Reflections on Language* (1975), *The Science of Language* (2012) | Language faculty is part of human biology; evolved recently; genetic basis (e.g., FOXP2 studies) | Interdisciplinary field connecting linguistics, biology, psychology, and evolution |
| **1960s–Present** | **Critique of Behaviorism** | Rejection of stimulus-response models of language | *Review of Skinner’s Verbal Behavior* (1959) | Language cannot be explained by conditioning; requires internal mental representations | Helped spark the cognitive revolution in psychology |
| **1960s–Present** | **Philosophy of Mind & Epistemology** | Rationalist tradition in cognitive science | Various essays & debates | Innate structures constrain possible grammars; Plato’s Problem, Poverty of the Stimulus | Deep influence on philosophy, psychology, AI debates |

## Can Chomsky be categorized as someone from the symbolic view of Cognitive Science?

- Chomsky **does not commit** to the idea that mental computation must be *physically* symbolic (as in GOFAI AI); he sees the symbolic model as a *theoretical abstraction*.
- In the **Minimalist Program**, he sometimes frames the language faculty in terms of optimal design and interface conditions, which can be interpreted in a more abstract, less mechanistic way.
- He has been critical of connectionism and subsymbolic models when they lack explanatory depth or fail to capture the discrete, combinatorial nature of language.
