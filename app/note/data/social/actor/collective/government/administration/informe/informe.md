# Informe

> Studies of concrete government-wide administration systems: polity-by-polity,
> period-by-period characterizations of how each system coordinates its agencies.
> The element notes carry the kinds; the studies below carry the instances. Each
> study locates its system with the reference frames of the
> [characterization atlas](../atlas/index.html): pick the applicable frames,
> assign one value per axis, read the resulting configuration.

## How to study a system

> Take one polity and period, reconstruct its coordinating layer (centers,
> standards, shared services, authority distribution, oversight), and record its
> coordinates axis by axis. Compare configurations across cases; do not reduce a
> system to a single type or score. Method and caveats live in
> [Government-Wide Administrative System](note.html?n=social/actor/collective/government/administration/gov-wide-administration.md).

### Ancient and imperial systems

- Qin Dynasty, China (221–206 BCE) — standardization, territorial control, hierarchical authority unifying a state
- Han Dynasty, China (206 BCE–220 CE) — recommendation recruitment, ranked officials, provincial administration
- Mauryan Empire, India (c. 322–185 BCE) — revenue-centered rule, provincial oversight at imperial scale
- Roman Republic (509–27 BCE) — temporary officeholding, political careers, divided authority
- Roman Empire (27 BCE–476 CE) — provincial governance, revenue extraction, fiscal-military integration
- Wei state, China (220–265 CE) — formal rank classification, elite recruitment, status in careers
- Byzantine Empire (330–1453) — administrative continuity and fiscal capacity across transformations
- Tang Dynasty, China (618–907) — examinations, official ranks, centralized ministries
- Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) — specialist expertise, ministerial coordination, delegated provinces
- Song Dynasty, China (960–1279) — professionalizing officialdom, examinations, center-official relations
- Ottoman Empire (1299–1922) — palace-trained officials, territorial delegation, central control
- Ming Dynasty, China (1368–1644) — examination recruitment, supervision and control of officials
- Mughal Empire, India (1526–1857) — ranked service integrating personnel, revenue, territory

### Early-modern states

- Catholic Monarchy (1492–1812) — delegation across distant territories, overlapping jurisdictions, officeholding
- Habsburg Monarchy (1526–1918) — coordination across territories with distinct legal traditions
- Tokugawa Shogunate, Japan (1603–1868) — continuity and coordination without a merit civil service
- Qing Dynasty, China (1644–1912) — imperial personnel system alongside provincial variation
- Ancien Régime France (before 1789) — fragmented authority, officeholding privilege, centralizing reform
- Revolutionary and Napoleonic France (1789–1815) — standardized territorial administration, prefects, careers

### Modern formation

- Prussia (17th–19th centuries) — examinations, career development, discipline, civil service founding
- British Empire (17th–20th centuries) — direct and indirect rule, differentiated territorial administration
- United States (1789–present) — career service with political appointees, fragmentation, federal coordination
- Norway (1814–present) — ministerial steering, professional administration, local autonomy
- Uruguay (1828–present) — public service modernization and career management in Latin America
- Italy (1861–present) — fragmentation, regional variation, central-local coordination
- Austria-Hungary (1867–1918) — coordination under divided political and administrative authority
- Canada (1867–present) — intergovernmental relations and shared federal-provincial responsibilities
- Meiji Japan (1868–1912) — administrative reorganization and professional bureaucracy in modernization
- German Empire and Weimar Republic (1871–1945) — professional continuity across political transitions
- Australia (1901–present) — federal-state coordination, central budgetary steering
- New Zealand (1907–present) — public management reform, departmental accountability, cross-agency coordination
- Republic of China (1912–1949) — coherence amid political fragmentation
- Soviet Union (1917–1991) — party-administration relations, personnel control, planning hierarchy
- Finland (1918–present) — ministries, autonomous agencies, municipal implementation
- Ireland (1922–present) — continuity, departmental coordination, parliamentary reform
- Thailand (1932–present) — bureaucratic continuity under political change
- Saudi Arabia (1932–present) — executive control, hierarchy, centralized coordination
- Iceland (1944–present) — capacity and service provision in a small state
- Germany (1945–present) — professionalism, legal constraint, federal distributed implementation
- India (1947–present) — permanent service across a large federal state
- Pakistan (1947–1971) — inherited personnel institutions in postcolonial state formation
