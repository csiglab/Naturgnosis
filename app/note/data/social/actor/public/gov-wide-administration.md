# Government-Wide Administrative System

> One instance, two readings. This note's primary belonging is the **social-collective** space: the **Government-Wide Administrative System** as the cross-government coordinating layer — the meta-administration observing, standardizing, and steering heterogeneous agency administrations. It is readable secondarily as an **activity** — the coordinating bundle through which that layer sets standards, runs shared services, monitors compliance, and reforms itself.

> A government-wide administrative system is generally **not a standalone system** with independent operational dynamics like a ministry. It is a reflexive layer over operational administrations: it shapes how the underlying administrative systems function without directly implementing services.

### What is this social instance?

> A government-wide administrative system fixes *how many administrations act as one government*: the scope of organizations and functions covered, the centers setting standards and running shared services, the distribution of authority between center and agencies, the common processes and infrastructures binding them, and the oversight and reform arrangements keeping the whole viable. Canonical instances: a national government's central personnel, budget, procurement, and digital administration spanning its ministries and agencies.

### What type of social element does this instance belong to?

**Government-Wide Administration belongs primarily to `Social Compound` as the government-wide kind of `Administrative Collective`** (`Collective (→ Kind) Administrative Collective (→ Kind) Public Administration (→ Kind) Government-Wide Administration`); see [Public Administration](note.html?n=social/actor/public/administration.md), the parent hub, and [Collective](note.html?n=social/collective/collective.md). It is typed under [Actor](note.html?n=social/actor/actor.md), and its participating units are [Public Organization](note.html?n=social/actor/public/public-organization.md) instances directed by a [Government](note.html?n=social/actor/public/government.md).

Its layer is **Ontic** where the system exists extra-mentally (centers, staff, shared platforms, budgets) with **Synontic** aspects (authority, standards, and compliance exist through legal warrant and shared recognition).

It is not an `Organizational Administration` (the internal administration of one agency), not the `Government` (the mandate-bound governing collective it serves), not an `Institution` (the stabilized rules it hosts — standards and classifications belong in the governance branch by reference), and not the `Workforce` (the administered agents — decompose the office where the mandate lives, the person where the capability lives).

### Is the type of this `social element instance` unambiguous?

> No, and the ambiguity is recorded as two trees, not a compromise type. The collective reading (the coordinating layer composed of centers, standards, and shared services) and the activity reading (the standard-setting, service-running, monitoring, reforming bundle) are both confirmed, so this note is multi-root per the ambiguity reading. No row carries two types; the trees cross-link through participation.

### Which facet does this element belong to, if applicable?

> Administrative first (cross-agency coordination into coherent government operation is the dominant causal mechanism); political where it distributes authority between center and agencies; legal-constitutional where mandate and competencies are defined; economic where it governs budgets, procurement, and shared-service resourcing. See [Facet](note.html?n=social/framework/facet.md).

## QA

### Is an administrative system even a system?

> In the architecture sense, yes as a conceptual system — but the primary unit being built, the network of administered actors, is not itself operationally self-contained. Call the coordinating layer a system where centers, standards, and services genuinely hang together; where they do not, name the fragmentation instead of overclaiming systemhood.

### Is the meta-public administration a system?

> Generally not a standalone system with independent operational dynamics like a ministry. It is a complementary higher-order layer observing, analyzing, integrating, and guiding multiple operational subsystems. Standard administrations execute policies, manage resources, and deliver services; meta-administration shapes how those subsystems function.

### Is public administration generic or agency-specific?

> Both, at different layers. The generic layer carries core processes, governance mechanisms, accountability structures, and HR rules; the agency-specific layer carries operational methods, domain workflows, and stakeholder practices. Some HR models are irreducibly agency-specific — the performance model for researchers differs fundamentally from that for police officers — so generic standards must leave room for domain logics.

### Why is Public Administration a misleading term?

> It implies the public sector can be administered as a single coherent object. In reality the public sector is a heterogeneous assemblage of agencies with distinct mandates, goal functions, metrics, constraints, and professional logics. What the term gestures at government-wide scale is better understood as meta-administration: coordination mechanisms, standards, shared services, and oversight architectures under which differentiated regimes coexist and align where necessary.

### How should the workforce in public administration be conceptualized?

> As the administered agent set, not the administration. The workforce agents are staffed through [Civil Service](note.html?n=social/actor/public/civil-service.md) arrangements, supplied through [Human Resource Unit](note.html?n=social/actor/public/catalog/hru.md) services, and occupied as [Public Employee](note.html?n=social/actor/public/catalog/employee.md) positions. Personnel detail lives in those entries, not here.

### Which Types of Elements Are Used to Construct the Dimensions of a Government-Wide Administrative System Characterization?

> The characterization system is constructed by defining dimensions that describe the functions, structure, organization, operation, and performance of a `Government-Wide Administrative System`. These dimensions are built from different types of elements, each capturing a distinct aspect of the system.

> These twelve element types are complementary rather than mutually exclusive categories, providing distinct but interconnected analytical perspectives from which characterization dimensions are constructed by examining the functions, structures, positions, roles, relationships, processes, mechanisms, resources, capabilities, rules, properties, and performance of individual system components and the administrative system as a whole.

| Element Type | Definition | What It Characterizes | Instance(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional Element** | A function the system performs or is expected to perform. | The functions and responsibilities of the administrative system. | Budget management; personnel administration; public procurement. |
| **Structural Element** | An organizational component of the system. | The institutions and organizational units through which administration is organized. | Ministry; central management agency; department. |
| **Positional Element** | An established position within the administrative system. | The formal positions through which authority and work are distributed. | Permanent secretary; director; administrative officer. |
| **Role Element** | A set of responsibilities and expected contributions assigned to an actor or position. | The responsibilities and expected behavior of administrative actors. | Decision-maker; supervisor; technical adviser. |
| **Relational Element** | A relationship connecting two or more system elements. | The connections and dependencies among institutions, positions, and other components. | Reporting relationship; supervisory relationship; interministerial coordination. |
| **Process Element** | An ordered sequence of activities that produces an output or result. | How administrative work is performed and organized. | Recruitment process; budget formulation; procurement process. |
| **Mechanism** | An arrangement through which a particular administrative effect is achieved. | How the system coordinates, regulates, or enables its operations. | Delegation; performance appraisal; interagency coordination. |
| **Resource Element** | A resource available to support administrative operations. | The inputs available to perform administrative functions. | Personnel; financial resources; infrastructure; information. |
| **Capability Element** | An ability to perform a function or achieve a specified result. | What the system or its components are capable of accomplishing. | Policy analysis capability; financial management capability; implementation capacity. |
| **Rule Element** | A formal or informal requirement governing administrative conduct or decisions. | The rules and constraints governing administrative action. | Civil service regulations; procurement rules; administrative procedures. |
| **Property** | A characteristic describing an element or the system as a whole. | The attributes and configuration of the administrative system. | Centralization; formalization; specialization; professionalization. |
| **Performance Element** | A measure or result used to assess administrative operation. | The outputs, outcomes, efficiency, effectiveness, and quality of administration. | Processing time; cost per transaction; service quality; target attainment. |

### How Can a Government-Wide Administrative System Model and Its Submodels Be Used to Characterize an Administrative System?

> A **Government-Wide Administrative System Model** provides an overarching analytical framework for describing, classifying, comparing, and evaluating the administrative arrangements that operate across a government. Its submodels decompose the system into distinct analytical domains, such as institutional organization, functional allocation, personnel management, authority, coordination, regulation, shared services, digital infrastructure, and accountability. Together, the model and its submodels provide a systematic way to construct a multidimensional characterization of an administrative system.

### Why Is Model-Based Characterization More Useful Than Element-Based or Entity-Type-Based Characterization?

> Because it organizes characteristics around how the system's functions, relationships, and mechanisms operate rather than listing components or classifying entities — the composite-model approach to a Comparactive Representation System, systematized in [Philosophia Representationis Comparativae et Operis](note.html?n=meta/philosophia-representationis-comparativae-et-operis.md).

### How to Evaluate a Characterization System for Government-Wide Public Administration Systems?

> Establish criteria and methods for assessing the conceptual coherence, completeness, distinctiveness, operationalizability, comparability, reliability, and empirical validity of a characterization system for government-wide public administration systems.

> The general evaluation criteria are systematized in [Philosophia Representationis Comparativae et Operis](note.html?n=meta/philosophia-representationis-comparativae-et-operis.md); the table below lists only the government-wide-specific additions and does not restate them.

| Criteria | Description |
|---|---|
| **Theoretical Grounding** | The dimensions are supported by established theories, comparative research, or defensible conceptual reasoning about public administration. |
| **Observability** | The characteristics being assessed can be identified through legislation, administrative records, official documentation, surveys, or other empirical evidence. |
| **Data Availability** | Sufficient information exists to characterize the relevant systems across the intended countries, institutions, and periods. |
| **Cross-National Comparability** | Dimensions and categories have sufficiently consistent meanings across countries despite differences in legal systems, administrative traditions, and institutional structures. |
| **Temporal Comparability** | The system can characterize administrative arrangements at different points in time and distinguish genuine institutional change from changes in definitions or measurement. |
| **Level-of-Analysis Consistency** | Each dimension specifies whether it concerns the entire state apparatus, a ministry, an agency, a personnel category, or an individual position. Findings at one level are not automatically generalized to another. |
| **Formal–Practical Distinction** | The framework distinguishes legally prescribed arrangements from arrangements actually implemented in administrative practice. |
| **Sensitivity to Institutional Variation** | The framework captures meaningful differences between systems without exaggerating minor differences or overlooking important institutional distinctions. |
| **Normative Neutrality** | The characterization distinguishes descriptive institutional properties from judgments about whether those arrangements are desirable, effective, or legitimate. |
