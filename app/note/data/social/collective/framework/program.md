# Theoretical Framework Program

> Theoretical Framework -- 	An organized set of theories and concepts used to explain or investigate a domain.

> The Goal is the develop a set of theories (expalanation, technical, normative) etc -- about -- the Public Shared Action Space of Free Actors -- Specialy Polity.

## Core Element

**Public Shared Action Space** (*Public Shared Action Substrate*)

The shared space within which individuals and free actors formulate goals, identify problems, coordinate activities, establish relationships, and undertake collective action.

## Main Goals

### 1. (Theory) Explanation of the Public Shared Action Space

- Why does a Public Shared Action Space emerge?
- What functions and roles does it perform?
- How do these roles emerge, develop, and change?
- What are the scopes of the Shared Action Space?
- How do these scopes emerge, expand, contract, and interact?
- What are the dynamics governing the development and transformation of the Shared Action Space?

### 2. (Practice) Theory of Collective Action and Meta-Action

Develop a theory of action within the Public Shared Action Space, including:
 **Collective Action:** How individuals and free actors coordinate their activities to pursue shared or compatible goals.
- **Collective Foundation:** How the shared space itself is established, maintained, and transformed.
- **Meta-Action:** Actions directed at the shared space itself, including the modification of its rules, institutions, organizational arrangements, and conditions of collective action.

### 3. (Limitations) Epistemic Asymmetry and the Limits of Collective Action

Investigate the limitations of the Public Shared Action Space, particularly those arising from the epistemic asymmetry of social actors.

- What are the fundamental limitations of a Public Shared Action Space?
- How does the unequal distribution of knowledge, information, expertise, and understanding among social actors affect collective action?
- What problems arise from the limitations of shared knowledge, communication, coordination, and decision-making?
- How do these limitations constrain collective governance?
- How can effective and efficient systems of collective governance be designed despite these constraints?

### 4. Intellectual Research Areas

- Cameralist Research
- Gubernandy Research

### 5. Theoretical Scope

**Can this research program provide the theoretical foundations for understanding the polity, the state, and public collectives more generally?**

Investigate whether the theory can explain the emergence, organization, operation, governance, and transformation of these collective entities.

## Subgoals

### 1. Develop a Deep Theory of Government, Collectives, Action, and the Polity

Develop an integrated theoretical framework that explains:

- **Collective:** The constitution, structure, boundaries, and persistence of collectives.
- **Action:** The formation of goals, the identification of problems, the development of solutions, and the coordination of activities.
- **Collective Action:** The processes through which multiple actors pursue shared, compatible, or conflicting goals.
- **Organization:** The arrangements through which actors, roles, resources, and activities are coordinated.
- **Governance:** The mechanisms through which collective action is directed, coordinated, constrained, and evaluated.
- **Polity:** The institutional and organizational structure through which a political collective constitutes and governs itself.
- **Government:** The actors, institutions, and processes through which a polity exercises governing functions.

### 2. Identify the Technical Domains Related to the Shared Action Substrate

Determine which technical domains are necessary to understand, organize, and transform the Public Shared Action Space.

Potential domains include:

- Governance
- Organization
- Problematization and Problem Formation
- Goal Formation
- Solution Generation and Development
- Decision-Making
- Collective Action
- Coordination
- Institutional Design
- Information and Knowledge Management
- Resource Allocation
- Evaluation and Learning
- Institutional Transformation

The objective is to establish how these domains relate to one another and how they jointly contribute to the constitution, operation, and transformation of the Shared Action Space.

### 3. Establish a Clear Conceptual and Naming System

Develop a consistent terminology for the research program, its central object, its theoretical components, and its associated research areas.

Questions to resolve:

- What should the overall research program be called?
- What is the most appropriate name for its central object: **Public Shared Action Space**, **Public Shared Action Substrate**, or another term?
- What is the relationship between the shared space, the collective, collective action, and government?
- What distinguishes the theory of the Shared Action Space from a general theory of collective action?
- What is the relationship between the general theory and its applications to the state, the polity, and other public collectives?
- How should the associated technical domains be classified and organized?

The goal is to establish a coherent conceptual architecture capable of supporting a unified theory of collective action and governance.

### Reality-Grounded Governance Theory


A theory of governance must derive its institutional models from an empirically defensible understanding of the reality being governed, the properties of the agents participating in governance, the complexity of collective problems, and the actual capabilities and limitations of available institutional arrangements. It must not depend on idealized assumptions about human knowledge, rationality, motivation, competence, or behavior that do not adequately correspond to reality.

Then the problem you are laying out is more specific: **how to design governance over an electorate whose members, by virtue of the complexity of the social problems being governed, will often possess only partial understanding of those problems—and whose majority may lack the understanding necessary to evaluate them adequately.**

Your concern is not simply how to educate voters, distribute knowledge, or improve representation. It is how to design a governance system that accounts for this persistent epistemic asymmetry.

The crucial design question becomes:

**What authority should electoral majorities exercise over decisions they may not sufficiently understand, and what institutional constraints, divisions of responsibility, and decision procedures should govern that authority?**

This opens several distinct design problems:

1. **Scope of electoral authority:** Which decisions should be determined directly by electoral preferences, and which require additional forms of assessment?
2. **Epistemic requirements:** How should decisions be handled when their technical or analytical requirements exceed the understanding of most electors?
3. **Delegation of judgment:** How should authority be distributed between elected representatives, specialized institutions, experts, and administrative organizations?
4. **Protection against epistemically defective decisions:** What safeguards should apply when a majority supports a decision based on materially incorrect assumptions or an inadequate understanding of its consequences?
5. **Accountability:** How can specialized decision-makers be held accountable without requiring the electorate to reproduce their expertise?
6. **Institutional competence:** How should governance arrangements ensure that decisions are informed by the knowledge and capabilities appropriate to the problems being addressed?

There is an important distinction here: **the problem is not that the majority is necessarily wrong, but that majority support and adequate understanding are different properties of a decision.** Neither guarantees the other.

Consequently, electoral authorization and the production of sufficiently informed decisions need to be treated as distinct functions of governance.

The design challenge is to establish a defensible relationship between them without assuming either that electoral majorities are epistemically infallible or that technical expertise, by itself, confers legitimate political authority.

That, as I understand your clarification, is the governance problem you are developing.
