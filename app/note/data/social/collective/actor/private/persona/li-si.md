# Li Si

> Li Si (李斯; c. 280 – 208 BC) was a Chinese calligrapher, philosopher, and official of the Qin dynasty. He served as Chancellor of Qin from 246 to 208 BC, first under King Zheng — the future Qin Shi Huang — and then under Qin Er Shi, and drove the administrative and cultural standardization of the newly unified empire.

| **Contribution** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Imperial standardization** | Assisted the First Emperor in unifying laws, governmental ordinances, weights and measures, and the gauges of chariots and carts, and made the Qin small seal script the imperial standard, proscribing variant scripts — a unifying effect on Chinese culture lasting millennia. |
| **Meritocratic government** | Built a government based on merit, so that imperial sons and younger brothers were not ennobled while meritorious ministers were; pacified the frontier regions to the north and south. |
| **Legalist statecraft** | In the *Shiji*, Li Si admired and used the administrative technique of Shen Buhai and Han Fei, while on law he followed Shang Yang; he had the feudal states' metal weapons melted into bells and statues, lowered taxes, and eased some of Shang Yang's draconian punishments. |
| ***Cangjiepian*** | Author of the *Cangjiepian*, the first Chinese language primer of which fragments survive. |
| **Suppression of dissent** | Persuaded the Emperor to restrict political books to the state and to pen the 213 BC edict ordering the destruction of historical records and literature, including key Confucian texts; the episode is traditionally tied to the "burning of books and burying of scholars". |
| **Succession plot and death** | After Qin Shi Huang's death, Li Si and the eunuch Zhao Gao suppressed the late emperor's chosen successor Fusu and installed Qin Er Shi; Zhao Gao later charged Li Si with treason and in 208 BC had him executed by waist chop, his family exterminated to the third degree. |

> A native of Chu and a student of the Confucian Xunzi, Li Si moved to Qin to advance his career, having concluded from the "rats in the granary and the outhouse" that people's worth is determined by their situation rather than fixed standards. Sima Qian records his last words: "I wish that you and I could take our brown dog and go out through the eastern gate of Shang Cai to chase the crafty hare. But how could we do that!"
