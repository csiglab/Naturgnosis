# Mozi

> Mozi (墨子; personal name Mo Di, 墨翟; c. 470 – c. 391 BCE), "Master Mo", was a Chinese philosopher, logician, and the founder of Mohism, one of the most prominent organized schools of the Warring States period. A carpenter and skilled device-maker of the lower artisan class, he and his followers argued against both Confucianism and Daoism for a philosophy of universal love, social order, frugality, and the will of Heaven.

| **Doctrine** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Universal love (*jian'ai*)** | People in principle should care for all others equally — "inclusive care" — against the Confucian view that one rightly cares for people in different degrees; close readings note it does not require renouncing family, and the Mohists held that "we begin with what is near." |
| **Condemning aggression** | Opposed expansionist war, which taxed populations and disrupted farming, treating it as robbery and murder on a grand scale; Mozi once walked ten days to Chu and defeated the strategist Gongshu Ban in nine simulated war games to forestall an attack on Song. |
| **Promoting the worthy / identifying upward** | Meritocratic government: rulers should hire competent, worthy subordinates rather than friends and relatives, and subordinates should model their conduct on morally competent superiors. |
| **Moderation in use and burial** | Frugality as the rule — utility is the only consideration for one's projects; lavish funerals and mourning rituals that bankrupted clans and disrupted farming were denounced, as was costly state music on the same economic grounds. |
| **Heaven's intent and ghosts** | Heaven (*tian*) is a benevolent moral force that rewards good and punishes evil, with ghosts and spirits as its enforcers; even if spirits did not exist, communal sacrificial gatherings would strengthen social bonds. |
| **Anti-fatalism** | Rejected the Confucian idea of destiny; hard work and virtuous acts could change one's position, and the "three-prong method" judged claims by their causes, functions, and historical bases. |
| **Consequentialist ethics** | Mohist ethics judges an action by its consequences for the benefit of all society — order, material wealth, and population growth — described by the Stanford Encyclopedia as the world's earliest form of consequentialism. |

> The ten core Mohist doctrines — promoting the worthy, identifying upward, universal love, condemning aggression, moderation in use, moderation in burial, Heaven's intent, understanding ghosts, condemning music, and condemning fatalism — are each justified as producing the best consequences for society. The *Mozi* is an anthology compiled over centuries by his followers; Mohism was suppressed under the Qin and died out under the Han, though its epistemology and philosophy of language left a lasting mark on classical Chinese thought.
