# Public Organization

> A **Public Organization** is an organization wielding delegated public authority under law — the government subtype of [Organization](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/organization.md), spanning ministries, councils, delivery and research bodies, task groups, arm's-length agencies, state-owned enterprises, municipalities, and public-private hybrids.

## Formulation

### What social element type does this social instance belong to?

**Public Organization belongs to the `Collective / Organization` social element type — a compound Interaction Unit with identity, membership, and rules, carrying its own agencies, roles, and practices.**

It is the government subtype of Organization: what individuates it is not its internal machinery (shared with every organization) but its constitutive relation to public authority — created or chartered under law, directed toward public purposes, and answerable through political oversight. Its layer is **Ontic**: ministries, agencies, and municipal bodies exist extra-mentally, with staff, premises, budgets, and address. Its facet is **political** — its dominant causal mechanism is authoritative rule-execution. Legal-instrument readings (statutes, charters) and economic readings (fees, commercial revenue) are prose here, not separate trees.

### What is this social instance?

> A public organization fixes *who exercises public authority and how*: the legal charter, the political principal to whom it answers, the agencies that form intention and select action, the internal governance stabilizing coordination, and the recurrent practices through which it serves, regulates, or trades. Canonical instances: ministry, council, implementation organization, policy research organization, task group, public agency, state-owned enterprise, municipal organization, nonprofit public-private organization.

## QA

### What Is a Characterization System for the `Public Organization Space`?

> `A Characterization System` for the `Public Organization Space` is a structured conceptual and methodological system for identifying, describing, distinguishing, classifying, and comparing the organizations that constitute the Public Organization Space.

> The resulting characterization should enable individual organizations to be described consistently, organizational types to be distinguished systematically, and the organization space to be analyzed as a structured domain.

> The general doctrine — general form of a characterization system, element derivation, concrete methods, and evaluation criteria — is systematized once in [Philosophia Representationis Comparativae et Operis](note.html?n=meta/philosophia-representationis-comparativae-et-operis.md). ### How do autonomous statutory, incorporated-administrative, executive-agency, Swedish-agency and crown-corporation forms differ?

> Along the five dimensions above: the **Autonomous Statutory Public Agency** draws autonomy from its own statute and legal personality; the **(Japan) Incorporated Administrative Agency** trades incorporation for bounded autonomy inside minister-set objectives; the **UK Executive Agency** trades a framework document for operational autonomy without separate legal personality; the **Swedish Government Agency** is constitutionally insulated from individual ministerial command while remaining under collective government direction; the **Crown Corporation** sits at the commercial pole — Crown-owned, board-governed, revenue-funded — bordering the Private Organization reading.

### What types of board composition structures exist?

> The board element itself — its kinds, anatomy, and terminology — is documented at [Board](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/board.md). This section keeps the public-organization-specific composition types.

| **Board Composition Type** | **Defining Composition Principle** | **What Types of Organizations Is It Best Suited For?** |
|---|---|---|
| **Public-Authority Board** | Representation of public authorities responsible for policy, oversight, or public accountability. | Agencies exercising sovereign powers or implementing government policy. |
| **Expert Board** | Selection based primarily on specialized professional or technical expertise. | Research institutions, technology agencies, and specialized evaluation bodies. |
| **Industry-Representative Board** | Representation of firms, industries, or business associations. | Industrial development, productivity improvement, technology adoption, and export promotion agencies. |
| **User-Representative Board** | Representation of the organizations or populations that use the agency's services. | Enterprise-support agencies, shared-service institutions, and user-oriented public organizations. |
| **Public–Private Board** | Representation of both public authorities and private-sector interests. | Applied technology, industrial development, and competitiveness institutions. |
| **Expert–Industry Board** | Combination of independent expertise and industry representation. | Applied research, technology transfer, and commercially oriented innovation institutions. |
| **Tripartite Board** | Representation of three institutional constituencies, typically government, industry, and academia or research. | Innovation systems, skills development, industrial research, and ecosystem coordination institutions. |
| **Investor-Representative Board** | Representation of investment expertise and capital-provider interests. | Public investment funds, development finance vehicles, and investment institutions. |
| **Multi-Stakeholder Board** | Representation of multiple constituencies affected by or involved in the organization's mandate. | Organizations with broad socioeconomic mandates or multiple stakeholder groups. |
| **Independent Board** | Emphasis on member independence from management, controlling interests, or other parties capable of compromising oversight. | Organizations requiring strong oversight, impartial judgment, and protection against conflicts of interest. |

### How Should Board Members Be Selected?

> Board-member selection should be based on the organization's mandate, governance requirements, required competencies, representation needs, and the degree of independence necessary for effective oversight.

> A selection system should establish **who is eligible, which qualifications are required, how candidates are identified and evaluated, who makes the appointment, and how conflicts of interest are managed.**

### Which Principles Should Guide Board-Member Selection?

> hese principles may sometimes conflict. For example, stakeholder representation can improve legitimacy but may complicate independence. The selection system should make such trade-offs explicit rather than assuming that every principle can be maximized simultaneously.

| **Principle** | **Guiding Requirement** |
|---|---|
| **Competence** | Candidates should possess the knowledge, skills, experience, and judgment required to fulfill their responsibilities. |
| **Merit** | Selection should be based on demonstrable qualifications and suitability rather than political allegiance or personal connections. |
| **Mandate Alignment** | Board composition should reflect the organization's legal mandate, strategic objectives, and institutional responsibilities. |
| **Functional Complementarity** | Collectively, board members should provide complementary competencies rather than unnecessary duplication. |
| **Independence** | Members responsible for oversight should be able to exercise impartial judgment without undue influence. |
| **Representation** | Where relevant, board composition should incorporate the perspectives of designated constituencies and affected stakeholders. |
| **Integrity** | Candidates should meet appropriate ethical standards and disclose relevant interests, affiliations, and potential conflicts. |
| **Transparency** | Selection criteria, procedures, responsibilities, and appointment decisions should be documented and sufficiently transparent. |
| **Accountability** | Appointing authorities and selection bodies should be accountable for following established procedures and justifying their decisions. |
| **Proportionality** | Selection requirements should reflect the complexity, risks, authority, and responsibilities of the organization. |
| **Continuity and Renewal** | Appointment terms, succession planning, and renewal procedures should preserve institutional knowledge while enabling periodic renewal. |
| **Diversity of Perspective** | The board should incorporate relevant differences in professional background, institutional experience, and perspectives that improve deliberation. |

### Which Comprehensive Methods Should Be Used?

> A rigorous selection system should combine several complementary methods rather than rely on a single appointment procedure.

| **Method** | **Purpose** | **How It Is Applied** |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Board Competency Mapping** | Determine the competencies required across the board. | Translate the organization's mandate and responsibilities into a competency framework and identify collective capability gaps. |
| **2. Board Composition Analysis** | Determine the appropriate composition of the board. | Establish the required balance of expertise, independence, institutional representation, and stakeholder perspectives. |
| **3. Role and Position Specification** | Define the requirements for individual board positions. | Specify responsibilities, required qualifications, desirable experience, independence requirements, and expected contributions. |
| **4. Eligibility Screening** | Exclude candidates who do not meet mandatory requirements. | Verify qualifications, legal eligibility, professional standing, and applicable incompatibility restrictions. |
| **5. Open Call for Candidates** | Expand access to qualified candidates. | Publicize vacancies and selection criteria through appropriate professional, academic, public-sector, and industry channels. |
| **6. Stakeholder Nomination** | Identify candidates with relevant institutional knowledge or constituency legitimacy. | Invite nominations from designated institutions, professional associations, research organizations, or other relevant constituencies. |
| **7. Professional Search** | Identify qualified candidates who may not apply through open calls. | Conduct structured searches through professional networks, specialist recruitment organizations, and relevant institutional directories. |
| **8. Structured Competency Assessment** | Evaluate candidates against predefined requirements. | Use standardized scoring rubrics, structured interviews, documented evidence, and role-specific assessment criteria. |
| **9. Independence and Conflict-of-Interest Assessment** | Evaluate whether candidates can exercise impartial judgment. | Examine relevant affiliations, financial interests, professional relationships, and other potential sources of conflicts. |
| **10. Collective Board Assessment** | Determine whether the proposed board works effectively as a whole. | Assess competency coverage, gaps, duplication, representation, and the interaction of members' backgrounds. |
| **11. Comparative Candidate Evaluation** | Establish a defensible ranking of eligible candidates. | Compare candidates using weighted criteria, minimum thresholds, and documented evidence. |
| **12. Independent Selection Committee** | Reduce arbitrary or politically motivated selection. | Assign candidate assessment to a suitably qualified committee operating under defined rules and conflict-of-interest safeguards. |
| **13. Appointment and Confirmation** | Complete the legally required appointment process. | Submit recommendations to the authorized appointing body and follow applicable approval or confirmation procedures. |
| **14. Post-Appointment Evaluation** | Determine whether the selection system produces an effective board. | Review board performance, competency gaps, independence, attendance, deliberation quality, and governance outcomes. |

### How Should These Methods Be Integrated into a Comprehensive Selection System?

> A defensible selection process can be organized into eight stages.

| **Stage** | **Guiding Question** | **Principal Output** |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Mandate Analysis** | What responsibilities must the board fulfill? | Board responsibility and governance requirements. |
| **2. Composition Design** | What combination of competencies, representation, and independence is required? | Target board composition profile. |
| **3. Position Specification** | What requirements should each position satisfy? | Board-position profiles and selection criteria. |
| **4. Candidate Identification** | How should qualified candidates be found? | Candidate pool generated through appropriate recruitment and nomination channels. |
| **5. Candidate Assessment** | How should candidates be evaluated and compared? | Documented assessments and comparative rankings. |
| **6. Collective Composition Assessment** | Does the proposed board satisfy the overall composition requirements? | Recommended board composition and identified gaps. |
| **7. Appointment** | Who has the legal authority to appoint or confirm candidates? | Formally appointed board. |
| **8. Evaluation and Renewal** | Does the board perform effectively, and what changes are needed? | Performance assessment, succession plan, and revised composition requirements. |

### Which Methods Should Be Used to Select Executive Leadership?

| **Category** | **Method** | **Purpose** | **Application** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Candidate sourcing** | Open recruitment | Attract candidates from a broad applicant pool. | Publish vacancies and invite applications from eligible candidates. |
| | Internal recruitment | Identify qualified internal candidates. | Recruit from the organization's existing workforce and management. |
| | Executive search | Identify qualified external candidates. | Search professional networks, industries, academia, and public administration. |
| | Institutional nomination | Obtain candidates through established institutions. | Invite nominations from universities, professional associations, and relevant public bodies. |
| **Candidate assessment** | Structured interview | Assess leadership capabilities consistently. | Evaluate strategic judgment, decision-making, and organizational leadership using standardized questions. |
| | Achievement assessment | Verify demonstrated professional capability. | Examine previous results, individual contributions, and the conditions under which achievements occurred. |
| | Strategic case assessment | Evaluate strategic reasoning and problem-solving. | Ask candidates to analyze an organizational challenge and defend a proposed strategy. |
| | Leadership simulation | Observe executive behavior under realistic conditions. | Simulate crises, resource-allocation decisions, and organizational challenges. |
| | Reference and integrity checks | Verify professional credibility and ethical suitability. | Confirm qualifications, employment history, references, and relevant conflicts of interest. |
| **Candidate evaluation** | Competency-based evaluation | Measure suitability against role requirements. | Assess strategic, managerial, technical, financial, and interpersonal competencies. |
| | Comparative merit ranking | Establish candidates' relative suitability. | Score candidates against predefined criteria and apply established ranking rules. |
| | Algorithmic evaluation | Apply systematic computational assessment. | Use predefined rules or models to evaluate and rank candidates. |
| **Final selection** | Board discretion | Allow the board to exercise judgment. | The board chooses a candidate from the eligible pool. |
| | Committee selection | Delegate the selection decision to a designated committee. | The committee selects or recommends a candidate according to its authority. |
| | Merit-based selection | Select according to demonstrated qualifications. | Appoint the highest-ranked eligible candidate under established rules. |
| | Random selection | Select randomly among eligible candidates. | Use a specified random procedure when candidates meet the required eligibility conditions. |
| | Stakeholder selection | Incorporate stakeholder participation. | Allow designated constituencies to participate in selecting the executive. |
| | Direct appointment | Allow an authorized authority to appoint a candidate directly. | The legally authorized body appoints a candidate without competitive recruitment, where permitted. |
| | **Random Selection Combined with Another Method** | Combine random selection with an additional selection method. | Apply random selection within a pool of candidates who meet predefined eligibility or merit criteria. |

### How Should the Selection Method Vary by Executive Position?

| **Executive Position Type** | **Particularly Important Selection Methods** | **Primary Evaluation Emphasis** |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy-Oriented Executive** | Structured interviews, strategic case assessment, institutional experience verification. | Policy judgment, strategic direction, and institutional coordination. |
| **Operational Executive** | Behavioral assessment, leadership simulation, achievement verification. | Execution, operational efficiency, and resource management. |
| **Scientific or Technical Executive** | Technical assessment, peer evaluation where appropriate, strategic case assessment. | Scientific or technical credibility, research leadership, and capability development. |
| **Regulatory Executive** | Structured interviews, legal and regulatory case assessment, integrity checks. | Regulatory judgment, impartiality, legal competence, and enforcement capability. |
| **Commercial or Investment Executive** | Investment case assessment, achievement verification, financial competency assessment. | Capital allocation, commercial judgment, financial performance, and risk management. |
| **Transformation Executive** | Organizational diagnosis, change-management case assessment, behavioral interviews. | Institutional reform, organizational development, and implementation of change. |
| **Research and Innovation Executive** | Technical and strategic assessment, achievement verification, stakeholder coordination assessment. | Research strategy, technological capability development, innovation management, and knowledge transfer. |
