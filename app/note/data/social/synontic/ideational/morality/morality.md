# Morality

> Morality is the system of principles and judgments, rooted in normative concepts of right and wrong, that governs intentional human behavior within a given ethical framework.

- How can it be known or justified? (epistemology)
- What should be done? (normativity)
- Why should it be done? (motivation)
- For whom and to what end? (scope and teleology)

## Term Linguistic

> **Common Ground:** Conduct, virtue, character, etc. Usually, it has both a descriptive and a normative component.

> In the Latin–Christian tradition, multiple aspects of human conduct—such as virtue, character, norms, and action—tend to be collapsed into the single term *morality*. In contrast, other traditions (e.g., Chinese, Indic, Islamic) differentiate these aspects into a more granular and structured set of concepts.

## Terminology

| **Term** | **Etymology** | **Description** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Moralis** | From Latin *mōrālis*, derived from *mōs* (*mōris*) meaning “custom,” “habit,” or “social usage.” | That which pertains to customs, habitual practices, and socially established norms of conduct; originally descriptive of social mores before becoming evaluative. |
| **Ethics** | From Ancient Greek *ēthos* (ἦθος), meaning “character,” “habit,” or “disposition,” systematized by Aristotle. | The philosophical discipline that systematically studies moral concepts, character formation, right action, justification of norms, and the structure of moral reasoning. |
| **Virtue** | From Latin *virtūs*, derived from *vir* (“man”), meaning “excellence,” “strength,” or “moral courage”; conceptually aligned with Greek *aretē* (ἀρετή). | A stable excellence of character that enables an agent to fulfill its function (ergon) well and to act in accordance with rational or moral excellence. |
| **Normativity** | From Latin *norma* (“rule,” “standard,” “carpenter’s square”). | The property of issuing or relating to standards, prescriptions, or “ought”-claims that guide evaluation, judgment, and action, rather than merely describing states of affairs. |
| **Justice** | From Latin *iustitia*, derived from *ius* (“law,” “right”). | The principle governing fairness, legitimacy, rightful order, and the proper distribution of rights, duties, or goods within a social or institutional structure. |
| **Hedonism** | From Greek *hēdonē* (ἡδονή), meaning “pleasure.” | The normative doctrine that pleasure (or the absence of pain) constitutes the fundamental good and the ultimate criterion of moral evaluation. |
| **Moral Framework** | From Latin *mōrālis* + Old English *framweorc* (“structure,” “construction”). | A structured and relatively comprehensive system of moral principles, values, assumptions about human nature, justificatory methods, and evaluative criteria that together guide moral judgment and institutional design. |
| **Moral Theory** | From Greek *theōria* (θεωρία), meaning “contemplation,” “systematic explanation.” | A formally articulated and philosophically defended account of moral principles and their justification, typically offering a general decision procedure (e.g., utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics). |
| Moral Value | From Latin *valēre* (“to be strong,” “to be worth”), via *valor* (“worth,” “importance”), combined with *mōrālis* (“pertaining to customs or conduct”). | A property, quality, or state of affairs regarded as worthy of approval, pursuit, or preservation within a moral domain; that which counts positively or negatively in ethical evaluation (e.g., well-being, autonomy, justice, honesty). Moral values function as evaluative standards that ground or inform normative judgments and moral reasoning. |

## Formulation

> What is the nature of morality as a target of study? What are the ontology and phenomenology, and what are the dynamics at each level?

> Morality can be studied on at least **five levels**: **biological, psychological, social/cultural, philosophical, and applied.** They interlock — biology sets constraints, psychology shapes mechanisms, society organizes norms, philosophy critiques them, and applied fields put them into practice.

| **Level** | **Field** | **Description** | **Questions** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neurobiological | Moral Neuroscience | Morality as emergent from neural circuits, brain regions, and biochemical processes underlying moral judgment and behavior. | Which brain areas are involved in moral reasoning? How do neurotransmitters influence ethical decision-making? |
| Individual | Moral Psychology | Morality as internal cognitive and emotional processes guiding personal judgments and actions. | How do individuals develop moral reasoning? What cognitive biases affect moral decisions? |
| Social | Social Norms & Ethics | Morality as shared rules, norms, and conventions regulating behavior in groups. | How are moral norms established and enforced? How does social context shape moral behavior? |
| Species | Evolutionary Ethics | Morality as adaptive traits evolved to promote cooperation and survival in humans and other species. | How do moral behaviors increase fitness? Which moral instincts are conserved across species? |
| Institutional | Legal & Organizational Systems | Morality embedded in laws, policies, and formal rules within organizations and societies. | How do institutions codify moral principles? When does legality diverge from morality? |
| Comparative | Cross-cultural Ethics | Morality as emergent patterns across societies, cultures, and historical contexts. | Which moral principles are universal? How do cultural contexts shape moral systems? |
| Normative | Philosophical Ethics | Morality as principles or frameworks prescribing what ought to be done. | What constitutes a morally right action? Are moral rules absolute or context-dependent? |

## Normation

> Deontology: In moral philosophy, deontological ethics or deontology (from Greek: δέον, ‘obligation, duty’ + λόγος, ‘study’) is the normative ethical theory that the morality of an action should be based on whether that action itself is right or wrong under a series of rules and principles, rather than based on the consequences of the action. It is sometimes described as duty-, obligation-, or rule-based ethics. Deontological ethics is commonly contrasted to consequentialism, virtue ethics, and pragmatic ethics. In this terminology, action is more important than the consequences.

## Moral Theory

> A **moral theory** is a **structured system** explaining what is morally right and wrong, why it is so, how we know it, and how agents should act based on that knowledge.

| **Theory** | **Core Principle** | **Key Thinkers** | **Evaluation Focus** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Deontology** | Morality is adherence to duty or rules. | Immanuel Kant | Rightness of the action itself |
| **Consequentialism** | Morality is judged by the outcomes of actions. | Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill | Goodness of the results |
| **Virtue Ethics** | Morality is developing virtuous character traits. | Aristotle | Moral character of the agent |
| **Contractualism** | Morality arises from social or hypothetical agreements. | Thomas Hobbes, John Rawls | Fairness of the social arrangement |
| **Care Ethics** | Morality is based on interpersonal care and relationships. | Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings | Quality of relationships and empathy |
| **Divine Command Theory** | Morality is obedience to divine will. | Various theologians | Conformity to God's commands |
| **Moral Relativism** | Morality depends on cultural or individual perspectives. | Franz Boas, Ruth Benedict | Context-specific moral standards |
| **Moral Realism** | Objective moral facts exist independent of belief. | Plato (early form), contemporary metaethicists | Objective truth of moral claims |
| **Moral Sentimentalism** | Morality is grounded in emotional responses. | David Hume, Adam Smith | Emotional approval or disapproval |
| **Egoism** | Morality consists in maximizing one's own self-interest. | Ayn Rand (ethical egoism), ancient skeptics | Agent’s benefit |

## Moral Framework

> A moral framework is a structured normative architecture that specifies **what counts as morally relevant**, **how moral claims are justified**, and **how action-guiding judgments are generated** across cases and institutions.

> Note: There are not moral entities - morality is a property of moral agent actions.

Four key questions:

- Which are moral agents?
- How to evaluate the morality of this action?
- How to guide actions in order to minimize in-morality?
- Grounding
    - How to ground this moral framework?

**Structure:**

- **Synontic Commitments**: *What exists morally?*

    (Moral facts, values, duties, rights, reasons, properties, or none.)

- **Metaethical Position**: *What makes moral claims true or valid?*

    (Realism, constructivism, expressivism, error theory, etc.)

- **Moral Anthropology**: *What kind of beings are moral agents and patients?*

    (Rational agents, sentient beings, socially embedded actors, vulnerable organisms.)

- **Fundamental Normative Principle(s)**: *What is the primary criterion of rightness or goodness?*

    (Utility, duty, virtue, rights, flourishing, systemic stability.)

- **Scope Condition**: *Who or what counts morally?*

    (All humans, citizens, sentient beings, future persons, ecosystems.)

- **Justification Method**: *How are principles defended or derived?*

    (Rational deduction, contractarian reasoning, reflective equilibrium, empirical performance, evolutionary explanation.)

- **Decision Procedure**: *How are concrete cases evaluated?*

    (Rule application, maximization, balancing, virtue judgment, case-based reasoning.)

- **Institutional Realization**: *How are norms implemented and stabilized socially?*

    (Law, governance, education, incentives, cultural transmission.)

- **Authority Condition**: *Why should agents comply?*

    (Rational necessity, mutual advantage, intrinsic value, coercive enforcement, identity coherence.)

- **Revision Mechanism**: *How does the framework adapt to new information or moral conflict?*

    (Deliberation, democratic processes, jurisprudence, philosophical revision, systemic feedback.)

## Principle Set

> Moral principles are fundamental beliefs or guidelines that help individuals distinguish right from wrong actions, guiding their behavior and decision-making in various aspects of life. Cultural, religious, philosophical, and personal beliefs often shape these principles and can vary widely among different societies and individuals.

 Some common moral principles include:

1. **Honesty**: Being truthful and transparent in one’s actions and words.
2. **Integrity**: Adhering to moral and ethical principles, even when no one is watching.
3. **Respect**: Acknowledging the worth and dignity of all individuals and treating them accordingly.
4. **Fairness**: Ensuring fair treatment for all without favoritism or discrimination.
5. **Responsibility**: Taking accountability for one’s actions and their consequences.
6. **Compassion**: Showing empathy and concern for the well-being of others.
7. **Justice**: Upholding principles of fairness and equity and working to correct injustices.
8. **Beneficence**: Actively doing good and contributing positively to the well-being of others.
9. **Non-maleficence**: Avoiding actions that cause harm or hurt to others.
10. **Autonomy**: Respecting the right of individuals to make their own choices and control their own lives.
11. **Accountability**: Taking responsibility for one's actions and decisions, including explaining and justifying actions to relevant stakeholders.

| **Morality** | **Description** | **Representative Philosopher(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Virtue Morality** | Focuses on the cultivation of moral character and excellence (*areté*). The good life is achieved through habituation of virtues. | **Aristotle**, Alasdair **MacIntyre** |
| **Deontological Morality** | Grounds morality in duty and universal rules independent of consequences. The moral act follows rational moral law. | **Immanuel Kant** |
| **Consequentialist / Utilitarian Morality** | Defines moral rightness by outcomes — actions are moral if they maximize overall good or happiness. | **Jeremy Bentham**, **John Stuart Mill** |
| **Divine Command Morality** | Moral norms are derived from divine will; goodness equals obedience to God’s commands. | **Thomas Aquinas**, William **Paley** |
| **Natural Law Morality** | Morality is grounded in human nature and the teleological order of the world; reason discerns natural moral law. | **Thomas Aquinas**, **Cicero** |
| **Hedonist Morality** | The good is pleasure; the moral life maximizes pleasure and minimizes pain (for the individual or group). | **Epicurus**, **Aristippus** |
| **Contractualist Morality** | Morality arises from rational agreements among agents seeking fair cooperation. | **Thomas Hobbes**, **John Rawls**, T. M. **Scanlon** |
| **Care Morality** | Emphasizes relationality, empathy, and care over abstract rules; morality as sustaining human interdependence. | **Carol Gilligan**, **Nel Noddings** |
| **Communitarian Morality** | Morality embedded in communal values and traditions rather than individual autonomy. | **Michael Sandel**, **Charles Taylor** |
| **Existentialist Morality** | Morality arises from authentic self-creation and responsibility; no pre-given moral order exists. | **Jean-Paul Sartre**, **Simone de Beauvoir** |
| **Sentimentalist Morality** | Moral judgments originate in moral feelings (sympathy, empathy) rather than reason. | **David Hume**, **Adam Smith** |
| **Nietzschean Morality** | Critiques herd morality; promotes the creation of values by the strong, life-affirming individual. | **Friedrich Nietzsche** |
| **Pragmatist Morality** | Morality evolves through social experimentation; what “works” for human flourishing is good. | **John Dewey**, **William James** |
| **Confucian Morality** | Morality as harmony, ritual propriety, and the cultivation of benevolent relationships. | **Confucius**, **Mencius** |
| **Stoic Morality** | Morality as living according to reason and nature, guided by inner virtue and apatheia (freedom from passions). | **Zeno of Citium**, **Epictetus**, **Marcus Aurelius** |
| **Utilitarian Altruism** | Emphasizes universal compassion and reduction of suffering, extended to all sentient beings. | **Peter Singer** |
| **Marxist Morality** | Moral values are historically conditioned by material relations; true morality promotes human emancipation. | **Karl Marx**, **Antonio Gramsci** |
| **Buddhist Morality** | Morality as right action within the Eightfold Path, aiming to reduce suffering and attachment. | **Gautama Buddha**, Nāgārjuna |
| **Postmodern Morality** | Rejects universal foundations; moral meaning is plural, contextual, and discursively constructed. | **Michel Foucault**, **Richard Rorty** |
