# Commerce

> Commerce matches supply with demand—and sometimes helps create demand—by organizing supply across wholesale, retail, marketplace, and intermediated channels to deliver products to consumers at the lowest feasible and progressively falling cost.

> **What is commerce?** Commerce is the economic activity of coordinating the exchange and distribution of goods and services between suppliers and demanders. In this note, we focus on commerce as a **supply–demand coordination problem**, in which a commerce firm intermediates between supply and demand.

Useful terms:

- **Problem Specification** → what problem needs to be solved.
- **Solution Requirements** → what the solution must achieve/satisfy.
- **Implementation Space**   → how the solution is actually configured, including financing, ownership, organizational, operational, technological, and contractual details.

> **Note:** Throughout this note, we use the concept of `space` as analogous to the mathematical concept of a space—as a guiding thinking mechanism, but without the corresponding mathematical rigor.

## Problem Specification

> What problem does commerce solve? In short — commerce solves at its core a [Supply–Demand Matching Problem](note.html?n=technique/retail-supply-demand-matching.md), worked here through its retail instance.

> **Note:** Matching supply and demand is the core problem, but it gives rise to a set of related problems that must also be addressed.

> **Note:** The `Commerce Problem` is an organizing abstraction over a set of related problems that can be derived from this table. It does not correspond to a single fixed problem specification; rather, it defines a **space of problem specifications**, with different commerce contexts instantiating different subsets and configurations of the related problems.

### What Is an Interaction Model, and How Does It Relate to the Commerce Problem Space?

> **An interaction model** is a configuration of actors, roles, and exchange relationships that connects a source of supply to a final customer. It specifies **which actors interact, the sequence of their interactions, and the functions performed at each stage** between production and consumption.

> The **commerce problem space** is the space of possible configurations for solving the problem of **matching supply with demand**. An interaction model is therefore a **solution-configuration dimension within the commerce problem space**: it defines the structure through which supply is connected to demand and determines which actors perform functions such as sourcing, aggregation, transformation, distribution, selling, and fulfillment.

> Different interaction models represent different ways of configuring the commerce system under different **requirements, constraints, resources, costs, risks, capabilities, and demand conditions**. The feasible interaction models are therefore a subset of the broader set of possible commerce solution configurations.

### Integral Business Model  (Solution) Space

> **What makes up a commerce business model?* -> Configuration of Implementation Dimensions.

> A commerce business model is the configuration of actors, activities, resources, relationships, and economic mechanisms through which a commerce firm matches supply with demand and creates, delivers, and captures value. More fundamentally, it specifies **which alternatives in the retail solution space the firm selects and how those choices are implemented and coordinated internally**.

> **Note:** These business models are not mutually exclusive or orthogonal to one another; they overlap and can be combined within a single commerce business. A business may therefore instantiate multiple business models simultaneously across different dimensions of its implementation.

> **Which business models currently exist?**

| **Business Model**               | **Description**                                                                                                 | **Concrete Commerce Problem Formulation**                                                                                 | **Implementation**                                                                                                                           |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Independent Retail**           | A retailer independently purchases and resells products to consumers.                                           | Independent retail commerce — match consumer demand with independently procured product supply.                           | Independent ownership · Retailer-owned inventory · Own procurement · Own pricing · Own retail channel · Merchant margin                      |
| **Specialty Retail**             | Focuses on a narrow product category or customer need.                                                          | Specialized retail commerce — match specialized customer demand with focused product supply.                              | Narrow assortment · Category specialization · Specialized procurement · Expert merchandising · Specialized sales channel                     |
| **General Merchandise Retail**   | Sells a broad range of unrelated product categories.                                                            | General merchandise commerce — match broad heterogeneous consumer demand with multi-category supply.                      | Broad assortment · Multi-category procurement · Centralized merchandising · Large inventory · General retail channel                         |
| **Department Store**             | Organizes broad merchandise into distinct departments within one retail operation.                              | Multi-category retail commerce — coordinate multiple product categories and customer demands within one retail operation. | Department structure · Shared infrastructure · Centralized purchasing · Category-specific merchandising · Physical retail                    |
| **Supermarket**                  | Retailer specializing primarily in food and household consumables.                                              | Grocery retailing — match frequent consumer demand with continuously replenished food and household supply.               | Food/household assortment · Retailer-owned inventory · Centralized procurement · Store network · Self-service · Frequent replenishment · POS |
| **Hypermarket**                  | Combines supermarket and general-merchandise retail at large scale.                                             | Large-format general retail — match broad consumer demand with high-volume multi-category supply.                         | Large-format facilities · Very broad assortment · Centralized procurement · High-volume inventory · Self-service                             |
| **Discount Retail**              | Competes primarily through low prices and cost-efficient operations.                                            | Price-sensitive retail — match demand while minimizing the total cost of retailing.                                       | Low-price positioning · Lean operations · Limited assortment · High volume · High inventory turnover · Cost-efficient procurement            |
| **Convenience Retail**           | Provides frequently purchased goods with emphasis on proximity and speed.                                       | Convenience retailing — satisfy frequent demand with geographically and temporally accessible supply.                     | Small-format stores · Dense locations · Limited assortment · High availability · Extended hours · Rapid transactions                         |
| **Warehouse Club**               | Sells goods to members, typically in bulk and at low margins.                                                   | Membership bulk retail — match price-sensitive demand with large-volume supply while reducing retail costs.               | Membership · Bulk assortment · Large-format warehouse · Low margins · High volume · Membership revenue                                       |
| **Cash-and-Carry**               | Customers purchase goods directly from a wholesale-oriented retail facility.                                    | Cash-and-carry commerce — match business/customer demand with bulk supply through customer-operated collection.           | Wholesale inventory · Self-service · Bulk purchasing · Customer pickup · Immediate payment                                                   |
| **Factory Outlet**               | Manufacturer or affiliated retailer sells products directly to consumers, often at reduced prices.              | Direct manufacturer retail — match consumer demand with manufacturer-controlled product supply.                           | Manufacturer supply · Direct retail channel · Outlet locations · Discounted inventory · Vertical integration                                 |
| **Franchise Retail**             | Independent operators run retail businesses under a common brand and operating system.                          | Distributed retail commerce — replicate a standardized retail solution across independently operated locations.           | Franchisor–franchisee structure · Brand licensing · Standardized operations · Distributed ownership · Franchise fees                         |
| **Cooperative Retail**           | Retail operations are collectively owned or controlled by participating members.                                | Member-oriented retail — coordinate member demand, procurement, and retail operations collectively.                       | Member ownership · Collective procurement · Shared governance · Member benefits · Cooperative operations                                     |
| **Direct-to-Consumer (DTC)**     | A producer or brand sells directly to the final consumer without traditional retail intermediaries.             | Direct producer commerce — match consumer demand directly with producer/brand supply.                                     | Producer-owned supply · Direct channel · Direct customer relationship · Direct pricing · Direct customer data                                |
| **Brand-Owned Retail**           | A brand operates its own retail channels to sell its products directly.                                         | Brand-controlled retail — coordinate product supply and consumer demand through proprietary channels.                     | Vertical integration · Brand-owned stores/e-commerce · Brand inventory · Proprietary channel · Direct customer data                          |
| **E-Commerce Retail**            | Products are sold to consumers through a digital storefront.                                                    | E-commerce retailing — match digitally expressed demand with available inventory and remote fulfillment.                  | Digital storefront · Online ordering · Digital payment · Central/distributed inventory · Delivery fulfillment                                |
| **E-Commerce Marketplace**       | A platform connects multiple sellers with consumers and facilitates transactions.                               | Marketplace commerce — match fragmented buyer demand with fragmented third-party supply.                                  | Multi-sided platform · Third-party sellers · Marketplace catalog · Platform payments · Seller network · Commission/fee revenue               |
| **Peer-to-Peer Marketplace**     | A platform enables individuals to transact directly with other individuals.                                     | C2C commerce — match individual sellers with individual buyers.                                                           | User sellers · User buyers · Platform intermediation · Reputation/trust system · Platform fees                                               |
| **Social Commerce**              | Commerce is conducted through social networks and social interaction mechanisms.                                | Social commerce — match demand and supply through social discovery and interaction.                                       | Social channel · Social discovery · Community interaction · Integrated purchasing · Social recommendations                                   |
| **Live Commerce**                | Products are demonstrated and sold through live digital interaction.                                            | Interactive commerce — match demand with demonstrated products through real-time digital interaction.                     | Live streaming · Host/presenter · Real-time interaction · Integrated checkout                                                                |
| **Omnichannel Retail**           | Integrates multiple physical and digital channels into a coordinated retail system.                             | Omnichannel commerce — coordinate demand across channels against shared supply and fulfillment resources.                 | Physical + digital channels · Shared inventory · Unified customer account · Cross-channel fulfillment · Integrated data                      |
| **Click-and-Collect**            | Customers order remotely and collect purchases at a designated physical location.                               | Hybrid fulfillment — coordinate remote ordering with customer-operated physical collection.                               | Digital ordering · Store/DC fulfillment · Pickup locations · Inventory reservation                                                           |
| **Subscription Retail**          | Customers pay recurring fees for recurring products or services.                                                | Recurring commerce — coordinate recurring demand with recurring supply and fulfillment.                                   | Subscription contract · Recurring billing · Scheduled fulfillment · Customer account                                                         |
| **Auto-Replenishment Retail**    | The system automatically initiates purchases based on consumption or predicted need.                            | Automated replenishment — satisfy recurring/latent demand through automatic purchasing.                                   | Consumption monitoring · Demand prediction · Replenishment rules · Automated ordering · Recurring fulfillment                                |
| **Membership Retail**            | Customers obtain access to products, prices, services, or benefits through membership.                          | Membership commerce — coordinate supply and demand through an access/membership relationship.                             | Membership system · Member pricing · Access benefits · Member account · Recurring fees                                                       |
| **Rental Retail**                | Customers obtain temporary use of products instead of purchasing ownership.                                     | Temporary-use commerce — match demand for product use with reusable provider-owned assets.                                | Provider-owned assets · Rental contract · Temporary possession · Return/redeployment · Usage fees                                            |
| **Leasing Retail**               | Products are provided for use over a contractual period in exchange for payments.                               | Contractual-use commerce — provide product utilization without transferring ownership.                                    | Lease contract · Scheduled payments · Provider asset ownership · Contractual usage                                                           |
| **Resale / Second-Hand Retail**  | Previously owned products are acquired and resold.                                                              | Secondary-market commerce — match demand with previously owned and heterogeneous supply.                                  | Product acquisition · Inspection · Valuation · Resale inventory · Secondary-market pricing                                                   |
| **Consignment Retail**           | A retailer sells goods on behalf of owners and receives a fee or commission.                                    | Consignment commerce — match demand with supplier-owned inventory while coordinating sales and settlement.                | Supplier-owned inventory · Retail selling · Commission settlement · Shared risk · Supplier reconciliation                                    |
| **Recommerce / Refurbishment**   | Used products are recovered, restored, and resold.                                                              | Recovery commerce — transform used products into commercially viable supply for secondary demand.                         | Product recovery · Inspection · Refurbishment · Resale inventory · Secondary-market distribution                                             |
| **Vending Retail**               | Automated machines provide products directly to consumers.                                                      | Automated point-of-sale commerce — satisfy localized demand without staffed retail operations.                            | Vending machines · Automated payment · Remote monitoring · Automated inventory replenishment                                                 |
| **Automated Store**              | A physical retail location uses technology to automate purchasing and fulfillment.                              | Automated physical retail — provide store-based commerce with reduced human intervention.                                 | Automated access · Sensors/computer vision · Automated checkout · Remote management                                                          |
| **Unstaffed Retail**             | A retail location operates without permanent on-site employees.                                                 | Unstaffed retail — provide physical retail access while minimizing on-site labor.                                         | Remote supervision · Automated access · Automated checkout · Remote operations                                                               |
| **Pop-Up Retail**                | Temporary retail locations are established for limited periods.                                                 | Temporary retail commerce — serve localized or time-limited demand without permanent facilities.                          | Temporary facility · Temporary inventory · Short deployment cycle · Flexible location                                                        |
| **Mobile Retail**                | Retail operations move between locations rather than operating from a fixed store.                              | Mobile retail commerce — bring supply to geographically distributed or changing demand.                                   | Mobile facility · Mobile inventory · Mobile POS · Flexible routing                                                                           |
| **Door-to-Door Retail**          | Products are sold directly through representatives visiting customers.                                          | Direct personal commerce — match customer demand through salesperson-mediated interaction.                                | Sales representatives · Direct ordering · Personal selling · Direct delivery                                                                 |
| **Catalog / Mail-Order Retail**  | Customers select products from a catalog and order remotely for delivery.                                       | Remote catalog commerce — match geographically distributed demand with centrally fulfilled supply.                        | Catalog · Remote ordering · Central fulfillment · Delivery                                                                                   |
| **Local Market / Bazaar Retail** | Multiple sellers offer goods directly to consumers in a shared marketplace.                                     | Local marketplace commerce — coordinate multiple local sellers with local consumer demand.                                | Shared marketplace · Independent sellers · Physical location · Customer traffic                                                              |
| **Farmers' Market Retail**       | Producers sell agricultural products directly to consumers through an organized market.                         | Agricultural direct commerce — connect local agricultural supply with local consumer demand.                              | Producer sellers · Temporary market · Direct sales · Local supply                                                                            |
| **Agent-Mediated Retail**        | An agent searches, selects, negotiates, or purchases products on behalf of a customer.                          | Delegated purchasing — match customer requirements with available supply through an agent.                                | Agent · Customer mandate · Supplier interaction · Delegated purchasing                                                                       |
| **Broker-Mediated Retail**       | An intermediary connects buyers and sellers and facilitates transactions without necessarily owning inventory.  | Intermediated commerce — match independent supply and demand without taking inventory ownership.                          | Buyer/seller network · Matching · Intermediation · Commission/fee                                                                            |
| **Personal Shopper Retail**      | A human shopper selects and purchases goods according to an individual customer's requirements.                 | Personalized delegated commerce — satisfy individualized demand through delegated product selection.                      | Personal shopper · Customer preferences · Delegated purchasing · Personalized fulfillment                                                    |
| **Concierge Retail**             | A provider performs sourcing, purchasing, coordination, and related services for a customer.                    | High-touch delegated commerce — coordinate complex customer requirements through a dedicated intermediary.                | Concierge agent · Supplier network · Delegated procurement · Personalized service                                                            |
| **On-Demand Retail**             | Products are sourced or fulfilled in response to specific customer demand rather than being stocked in advance. | On-demand commerce — satisfy demand without requiring equivalent inventory to be positioned in advance.                   | Customer order → sourcing/production → fulfillment · Low pre-positioned inventory · Demand-triggered procurement                             |

### Theoretical Formulation

> At its core, **commerce is the problem of allocating scarce resources to satisfy uncertain and heterogeneous consumer demand while maximizing long-term economic value**.

> **Given heterogeneous consumers, uncertain demand, scarce resources, competitors, multiple channels, and intertemporal decisions, what prices, products, inventory, locations, services, and channels should a commerce firm choose to maximize long-term economic value?**

> Let a commerce firm choose, over time, $a_t=(p_t,A_t,I_t,m_t,s_t,c_t,L_t)$
where $p$ is price, $A$ assortment, $I$ inventory, $m$ marketing, $s$ service, $c$ channel allocation, and $L$ location/network.

The commerce firm solves the dynamic optimization problem

$$
\boxed{
\max_{\{a_t\}}
E_0\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t
\Pi_t(a_t,S_t,\theta_t)
\right]
}
$$

subject to

$$
\boxed{
D_{jt}
=
D_j(p_t,A_t,m_t,s_t,c_t,L_t,
X_t,Z_t,P_{-t},\theta_t,\varepsilon_t)
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
I_{j,t+1}
=
I_{jt}+O_{jt}-Q_{jt}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Q_{jt}\leq D_{jt},\qquad
Q_{jt}\leq I_{jt}+O_{jt}
}
$$

and the commerce firm's profit is

$$
\boxed{
\Pi_t
=
\sum_j(p_{jt}-w_{jt})Q_{jt}
-C_t(I_t,A_t,m_t,s_t,c_t,L_t)
}
$$

where \(w\) is acquisition cost and \(C\) includes operating, inventory, fulfillment, marketing, labor, occupancy, and other costs.

Demand itself emerges from consumer choice:

$$
\boxed{
U_{ijt}
=
V(X_j,Z_i,A_t,c_t,L_t,s_t)
-\alpha_i p_{jt}
+\varepsilon_{ijt}
}
$$

so that consumers choose the alternative that maximizes utility.

Because competing commerce firms affect prices, assortment, locations, and consumer demand, the commerce firm's optimization is embedded in a **strategic equilibrium**:

$$
\boxed{
a_r^*
\in
\arg\max_{a_r}
E[\Pi_r(a_r,a_{-r}^*)]
}
$$

Finally, because demand, preferences, competition, costs, and technology evolve through time, the complete problem is a **dynamic stochastic optimization and equilibrium problem**:

$$
\boxed{
V(S_t)=
\max_{a_t}
\left\{
\Pi(S_t,a_t)
+
\beta E[V(S_{t+1})\mid S_t,a_t]
\right\}.
}
$$

Thus, the **science of commerce** can be summarized as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Consumer Choice}
\rightarrow
\text{Demand}
\rightarrow
\text{Price \& Assortment}
\rightarrow
\text{Inventory \& Operations}
\rightarrow
\text{Channels \& Location}
\rightarrow
\text{Competition}
\rightarrow
\text{Profit}
\rightarrow
\text{Long-Term Firm Value}
}
$$

### How is productivity measured in the commerce sector?

> Partial measures stay descriptive and live on ratios the firm already records: real sales per labor hour, per square meter, and per unit of inventory. Frontier methods (DEA/SFA; see Keh & Chu, 2003) render the firm as producing *transactions plus distribution services*, and scores there are only as honest as the output deflator behind them — a frontier over nominal sales ranks pricing power, not productivity. The level choice matters as much as the method: a firm-level score is not the sector score, because aggregate productivity moves through entry, exit, and reallocation as much as through within-firm improvement (Foster, Haltiwanger, & Krizan, 2002).

### What is the production function of commerce?

> Output is **real sales in constant prices** — the joint product of goods transferred and distribution services rendered. Inputs are labor, capital, and space: payroll hours, the deflated replacement cost of stores and inventory, and selling area. Commerce is a *service* production function: the transaction is the output, which is why margin-based and sales-based measures diverge (Bureau of Labor Statistics). A firm-level function is not the sector function: aggregation runs both directions, through reallocation across firms and through the household production of the shopper, which is an input the retailer's own books never record (Betancourt & Gautschi, 1990).

### How is the production function of commerce operationalized?

> The form is the easy part; the decisions below are what make an estimate mean anything. The baseline form is Cobb–Douglas, $Q = A L^{\alpha}K^{\beta}M^{\gamma}$ (labor, capital, materials/space), with CES when input substitution is the live question. Each row is where a fitted instance of the [Production Function](note.html?n=epistemica/production-function.md) epistemic blueprint either earns or loses its claim — see also the [production-function node](/epistemica/?node=production-function).

| Decision | The choice that matters | Commerce reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Output** | Deflate nominal sales by a retail-specific implicit price deflator. | Deflating by CPI confounds the firm's pricing power with its productivity; the sector-specific deflator is the Betancourt point (1993). |
| **Labor input** | Payroll hours, matched to traffic and workload, not headcount. | Split full-time/part-time and skill; "Revenue per labor hour" is $\alpha$ made visible. |
| **Capital input** | Deflated replacement cost of stores, equipment, and inventory. | Inventory is productive capital in commerce, not a buffer; "Sales per m²" is its partial reading. |
| **Functional form** | Cobb–Douglas baseline; translog when checking curvature and scale. | Unit substitution is usually fine at firm level; test it before assuming it. |
| **Estimator** | Log-linear OLS as description; IV once the deflator makes output endogenous. | Own-price in the deflator creates simultaneity with productivity; cost-shifters are the honest instruments. |
| **Standards** | Non-negativity of elasticities; constant-returns test; shares vs. operating costs. | Retail homogeneity should come out near-constant; if $\alpha$ diverges from labor's cost share, the output or the deflator is wrong, not the theory. |
| **Derived measures** | Labor elasticity, returns to scale $\alpha+\beta+\gamma$, the TFP residual. | The residual is managerial practice and assortment quality to the extent the inputs are measured right (Metcalfe, Sollaci, & Syverson, 2023). |

### Why can't a commerce firm cover the entire commerce problem space?

* Which **problem-configuration elements** impose constraints on the feasible solution configurations a commerce firm can implement?
* Do some problem-configuration elements create **implementation conflicts, incompatibilities, or trade-offs** with others?
* Do some problem-configuration elements create **complementarities or dependencies** between solution configurations?

> **Configuration Interaction** — a relationship between implementation choices in which the selection of one configuration affects the feasibility, cost, performance, or implementation difficulty of another.

> **Implementation conflict** — the implementation of one configuration increases the difficulty, cost, or constraints associated with implementing another.

> **Implementation Space** = the set of feasible configurations of implementation dimensions, subject to constraints and interactions among dimensions.

> **Note**: Conflict is an irreflexive, symmetric, non-transitive relation between configuration elements.

### Why Can't One Interaction Model Solve the Entire Commerce Problem Space?

> No single interaction model can solve the entire commerce problem space because the commerce problem contains **heterogeneous requirements, constraints, resources, demand conditions, products, geographic configurations, transaction frequencies, and risk structures**.
>
> An interaction model is therefore a **particular configuration of the solution space**, not a universal solution. Different configurations of supply, demand, distance, scale, perishability, information, capital, capabilities, and coordination requirements make different interaction structures feasible or useful.
>
> The commerce solution space therefore contains **multiple possible interaction models**, and a commerce system may combine several of them.

### What Does an Interaction Model Generate?

> An interaction model **generates and represents a division of labor among the actors participating in the commerce process**.
>
> By assigning different functions to different actors, it generates a **value-chain structure**: a sequence of specialized activities and exchanges through which a product moves from supply toward final consumption.
>
> Thus, an interaction model determines, at least structurally:
>
> **Actors → Roles → Functions → Exchanges → Division of Labor → Value Chain**
>
> For example:
>
> **Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer**
>
> implies a division of labor in which:
>
> * the **producer** performs production;
> * the **wholesaler** performs aggregation and distribution;
> * the **retailer** performs assortment, merchandising, and final sale;
> * the **consumer** performs the final consumption.
>
> The interaction model therefore does not merely describe **who sells to whom**. It specifies a particular **organizational decomposition of the commerce problem into specialized roles and interactions**.
