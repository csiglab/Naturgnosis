# Alexander Hamilton


> Alexander was a reader of **`Jean-Baptiste Colbert`.**
> 

> 

## Hamilton Reading Material

| Date of reading / contact | Document / work / person | Type | Economic / policy subject | What Hamilton appears to have taken from it | Evidence of Hamilton's engagement | Influence on Hamilton |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1770s, probably before 1777** | **David Hume, *Political Discourses* (1752)** | Book / political economy | Money, trade, public debt, balance of trade, commerce | Hume's analysis of money, trade and public debt; especially the relationship between public debt, circulation and commerce | Founders' Papers explicitly state that Hamilton had studied Hume's *Political Discourses* before entering Congress | **Very high** — one of Hamilton's most important early economic influences ([Founders Online][1]) |
| **1770s** | **James Steuart, *An Inquiry into the Principles of Political Oeconomy* (1767)** | Book / political economy | Money, coinage, banks, circulation, trade, public credit, taxation, industrial policy | A highly interventionist/systemic view of the monetary and economic system; currency, banking, circulation and state policy | Hamilton's later reports contain numerous parallels; editors conclude Steuart directly influenced the Mint report | **Very high** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1770s–1780s** | **Malachy Postlethwayt, *The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce* (1751/1757)** | Commercial encyclopedia / reference work | Trade, customs, commerce, money, exchange, manufactures, colonial economy | Practical commercial knowledge rather than simply theory; terminology, institutional descriptions, foreign trade and monetary information | Hamilton repeatedly used it; in 1781 he specifically asked Pickering to obtain it | **High**, particularly for empirical/commercial knowledge ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1770s–1780s** | **Wyndham Beawes, *Lex Mercatoria Rediviva* (1761)** | Commercial/legal reference | Merchant law, international commerce, trading companies, currencies, commercial institutions | Practical knowledge of international commercial institutions and law | Hamilton specifically requested it from Pickering in 1781 | **Moderate–high** ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1770s–1780s** | **Richard Price, *Observations on the Nature of Civil Liberty* (1776)** | Political/economic pamphlet | British taxation, public revenue, money, France's finances | Quantitative information about European fiscal systems and circulating money | Hamilton explicitly requested the work from Pickering in April 1781 and used Price's numerical estimates | **High for empirical fiscal information** ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1770s–1780s** | **Richard Price, *Additional Observations on the Nature and Value of Civil Liberty* (1777)** | Political/economic pamphlet | Public finance, taxation, money | Further fiscal and monetary statistics | Hamilton specifically requested either this or the 1776 work; he used Price's figures in his 1781 analysis | **Moderate–high** ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1770s–1780s** | **Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)** | Book / political economy | Division of labor, capital, banking, money, trade, agriculture, manufacturing, public finance | Smith supplied Hamilton with a sophisticated counter-position on free trade, banking and economic development. Hamilton frequently **used Smith's arguments while disagreeing with some of his policy conclusions** | Founders' editors identify Smith as one of the economic writers Hamilton studied before becoming Treasury Secretary | **Very high** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **c. 1780** | **Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations*, discussion of banking** | Specific intellectual input | Banking and productive capital | The idea that banking can transform otherwise idle money/capital into productive capital | Hamilton's later national-bank report closely parallels Smith's discussion; a copy of Smith was sent to Hamilton by Angelica Church | **Very high** for banking theory ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780–1781** | **British fiscal system / British financial literature** | Institutional knowledge + reports/books | Taxation, public debt, revenue, paper money, banking, public credit | Hamilton studied Britain as a functioning fiscal-financial system—not merely as a political enemy | His April 1781 letter to Robert Morris contains detailed estimates of British circulating cash and revenue | **Very high** ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1780–1781** | **French fiscal system / French financial literature** | Institutional knowledge | Revenue, specie circulation, public finance | Comparison between French metallic currency and British paper-credit system | Hamilton calculated French circulating cash and revenue in his 1781 letter to Morris | **High** ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1780–1781** | **Robert Morris** | Person / practical financial mentor | Public finance, government credit, banking, currency, war finance | The practical architecture of national finance; government credit as a political institution | Hamilton wrote Morris a remarkably detailed financial memorandum in 1781 and subsequently worked closely with him | **Very high** |
| **April 1781** | **Hamilton's financial memorandum to Robert Morris** | Original analytical document | Banking, currency, public revenue, national credit | Hamilton's own synthesis of European financial evidence; estimates of France, Britain and Dutch finances | Surviving letter contains extensive numerical analysis and references to Hume, Price, Smith and others | **Foundational** — demonstrates Hamilton already doing comparative political economy himself ([Founders Online][3]) |
| **1781** | **Banking institutions of Britain and Europe** | Institutional precedent | Banking, credit creation, circulation | Banks as instruments for mobilizing capital and supporting commerce | Hamilton wrote that commercial nations had found banks necessary and regarded them as powerful engines of trade | **Very high** ([JSTOR][6]) |
| **1780s** | **Bank of England** | Institutional model | Central/public banking, government debt, credit | The integration of government finance and banking; credibility of public debt | Explicitly discussed as an important European precedent in preparation of Hamilton's national-bank proposal | **Very high** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **Bank of Scotland / Scottish banking system** | Institutional model | Note issuance, banking, circulation, productive capital | Banking as a mechanism for increasing the active portion of capital | Smith's discussion of Scottish banking became directly relevant to Hamilton's banking arguments | **High** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **Jacques Necker, *A Treatise on the Administration of the Finances of France*** | Book / fiscal policy | Public finance, revenue, taxation, debt, specie, economic policy | The state as an active manager of national economic capacity; fiscal administration; diversified production | Hamilton referred to Necker explicitly and later drew heavily on him | **Very high** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s** | **Jacques Necker's writings/speeches on French finance** | Government policy documents / speeches | Public credit, taxation, economic administration | Practical French fiscal administration | Hamilton had referred to Necker before 1789; copies of Necker's speeches circulated in America | **High** ([Founders Online][1]) |
| **1780s** | **Necker on grain trade and manufacturing** | Economic policy analysis | Agriculture/manufacturing, trade restrictions, national self-sufficiency | The argument that a diversified economy could strengthen national power and the balance of trade | Strong conceptual parallels appear in the *Report on Manufactures* | **Very high** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s** | **Necker's discussion/eulogy of Colbert** | Historical economic policy | Mercantilism, manufacturing, state development | Manufacturing policy as an instrument of national power | Founders' editors identify parallels between Necker's discussion of Colbert and Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* | **High** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s** | **Sir James Steuart, monetary/coinage writings** | Economic theory | Money, coin, bullion, exchange, prices | Analysis of coin debasement, bullion, exchange rates and foreign trade | Hamilton's Mint report contains numerous parallels and directly cites Steuart | **Very high** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1780s** | **Adam Anderson, *An Historical and Chronological Deduction of the Origin of Commerce* (1764)** | Commercial history | Trade, commerce, coinage, British economic development | Historical data on British commerce and recoinage | Hamilton used Anderson in preparing the Mint report | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1780s** | **Pelatiah Webster, *An Essay on Credit* (1786)** | American economic pamphlet | Banks, credit, Bank of North America | American banking experience and arguments concerning bank credit | Hamilton's national-bank research engaged with Webster's arguments | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **John Witherspoon, *Essay on Money* (1786)** | American monetary pamphlet | Money, paper currency, banking | American debate over paper money and monetary circulation | Cited among sources relevant to Hamilton's banking analysis | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **Thomas Paine, *Dissertations on Government, the Affairs of the Bank, and Paper-Money* (1786)** | Political/economic pamphlet | Banking, paper money, government | Arguments in the American banking/paper-money debate | Appears among sources used in the documentary apparatus of Hamilton's bank research | **Moderate / contextual** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **John Law's financial system / Mississippi experience** | Historical institutional precedent | Paper money, banking, public credit, speculative bubbles | Negative lessons about uncontrolled credit and monetary expansion | Hamilton's bank research may have been informed by knowledge of Law's failure | **Moderate; evidence less direct** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1780s** | **Thomas Hobbes** | Political philosophy | Sovereignty, state authority, government | Conceptual justification for strong national authority | Later scholars have identified Hobbes as an important Hamilton influence, though this is more political than economic | **High politically; lower economically** ([Founders Online][1]) |
| **1780s** | **Emer de Vattel, *The Law of Nations* (1758)** | International law / political economy | Commerce, sovereignty, commercial restrictions | The idea that moderate commercial restrictions could coexist with natural law | Founders' editors identify Vattel as an influence on the *Report on Manufactures* | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s** | **Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748)** | Political philosophy / political economy | Commerce, political institutions, commercial freedom | A more qualified conception of commercial freedom; trade policy dependent on political circumstances | Hamilton's manufacturing arguments parallel Montesquieu's treatment of commerce | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s** | **Thomas Pownall, economic writings** | Political/economic writing | Value, productivity, agriculture vs. manufacturing | Ideas concerning productivity and the measurement of economic value | Hamilton's draft *Report on Manufactures* contains a formulation resembling Pownall | **Moderate / possible** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1780s–1790s** | **British Parliamentary debates / fiscal legislation** | Government documents / debates | Taxation, public debt, trade, banking | Actual policy experience rather than abstract theory | Founders' editors explicitly note that British financial experience was available through Parliamentary Debates and printed works | **Very high as institutional evidence** ([Founders Online][1]) |
| **1789–1790** | **U.S. Treasury / Congressional financial records** | Government documents | Federal debt, revenue, customs, state debts | Raw American fiscal data from which Hamilton constructed national financial policy | Hamilton's Treasury work itself involved collecting and analyzing extensive financial information | **Foundational** |
| **1790** | **Congressional order on manufactures, Jan. 15, 1790** | Policy directive | Industrial development | Formal mandate to investigate how U.S. manufacturing could be promoted | Became the basis for Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* | **Direct policy trigger** ([Founders Online][7]) |
| **1790** | **European mint and monetary systems** | Comparative institutional research | Coinage, mint charges, bullion, specie | Comparative design principles for a U.S. Mint | Hamilton repeatedly sought information on foreign mint practices during autumn 1790 | **Very high** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1790** | **British Mint / free coinage policy** | Institutional precedent | Coinage | Free coinage and low mint charges as a model for U.S. policy | Hamilton explicitly used British mint policy in his Mint report | **High** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1790** | **Sir Isaac Newton's 1717 mint/assay report** | Government/technical report | Gold/silver ratio, assay, coinage | Technical basis for monetary/coinage calculations | Hamilton used Newton's report through Postlethwayt | **High technical influence** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1790** | **Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations*, banking chapters** | Economic theory | Banking, capital, circulation | Banking can convert otherwise idle capital into productive capital | Hamilton's National Bank report parallels Smith closely | **Very high** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1790** | **European banking precedents generally** | Comparative institutional research | National banking | Institutional architecture for a national bank | Editors describe Hamilton as relying "heavily" on European precedents and banking theory | **Very high** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1791** | **Hamilton's *Report on the Establishment of a Mint*** | Hamilton's policy report | Coinage, money, exchange | Synthesis of Steuart, Postlethwayt, Smith, Newton and foreign institutional practice | Final report submitted Jan. 28, 1791 | **Hamilton's synthesis** ([Founders Online][2]) |
| **1791** | **Hamilton's *Second Report on the Further Provision Necessary for Establishing Public Credit*** | Policy report | National bank, credit, banking | U.S. national banking architecture | Submitted Dec. 13, 1790; prepared using European banking precedents and Smith | **Hamilton's synthesis** ([Founders Online][5]) |
| **1791** | **European manufacturing policy generally** | Comparative policy evidence | Tariffs, bounties, manufactures, industrialization | Argument for strategic industrial development rather than dependence on agricultural exports | Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* synthesizes decades of European policy debate | **Very high** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1791** | **Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations*** | Economic theory | Free trade, division of labor, manufacturing | Hamilton uses Smith extensively—but often to identify limits of Smith's conclusions under actual international conditions | Strong textual parallels; editors say Smith supplied material Hamilton refuted | **Very high, partly adversarial** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1791** | **Jacques Necker** | Fiscal/economic policy | Manufacturing, trade restrictions, national power | Probably the closest European intellectual analogue to Hamilton's industrial-policy position | Editors explicitly say Hamilton's conclusions were generally closer to Necker than Smith | **Very high** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1791** | **Sir James Steuart** | Political economy | Manufacturing, agriculture, population, economic policy | Economic development as something government could deliberately shape | Significant intellectual source for Hamilton's industrial and monetary thinking | **High** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1791** | **Vattel + Montesquieu** | International/political theory | Commercial restrictions | Intellectual justification for selective restrictions without abandoning commercial freedom | Identified by editors as influences on Hamilton's manufacturing policy | **Moderate** ([Founders Online][4]) |
| **1791** | **Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures*** | Policy report | Tariffs, bounties, industrialization, labor, machinery, agriculture/manufacturing | Hamilton's mature industrial-development strategy | Submitted Dec. 5, 1791 | **Hamilton's synthesis** ([Founders Online][7]) |

## Hamilton vs Colbert

| Dimension | Colbert's model | Hamilton's interpretation | Hamilton's policy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **State role** | Active state direction of economic development | Government should deliberately cultivate productive capacity | Federal economic policy |
| **Manufacturing** | Promote domestic manufactures | Manufacturing is essential to national power | *Report on Manufactures* |
| **Import policy** | Protect/domestically develop strategic industries | Tariffs can protect emerging American industries | Protective duties |
| **Subsidies** | Bounties and privileges for favored industries | Government encouragement can accelerate industrial development | Bounties/premiums |
| **Technology** | Attract foreign skills and improve production | Attract European artisans and technology | Immigration + machinery |
| **National power** | Commerce and manufacturing strengthen the state | Economic capacity is part of national security | Industrial independence |
| **Economic diversification** | Reduce dependence on foreign manufactures | Agriculture alone leaves the country dependent | Diversified economy |
| **Trade** | State-managed commercial strategy | Free trade cannot be assumed when other states pursue protection | Strategic commercial policy |
| **International competition** | France must compete with England and Holland | United States must compete with European commercial powers | National commercial strategy |
| **Long-term objective** | Make France a major commercial/manufacturing power | Make the United States economically independent and powerful | Hamiltonian economic program |
