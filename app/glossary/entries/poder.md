> The cross-linguistic pattern is not random—it is structurally consistent and reveals how different civilizations decomposed the concept of “power” into distinct semantic primitives.
> 

> At a deep level, languages tend to derive their primary term for “power” from one of two core semantic bases: **capacity (the ability to act)** or **force (the ability to impose)**. In Romance languages such as Spanish (*poder*) and French (*pouvoir*), the dominant lineage traces back to Latin *potere*, which encodes power as *capability*—a latent, modal property tied to what an agent can do. By contrast, Germanic languages like German (*Macht*) derive from Proto-Indo-European **magh-*, emphasizing *strength* or *effective force*, i.e., the realized ability to produce effects in the world.
> 

> A second layer of differentiation appears in how languages treat **authority as distinct from raw capacity**. Slavic languages such as Russian (*власть*) lexicalize power primarily as *rule or dominion*, separating it from physical strength. Similarly, Latin historically distinguished *potentia* (capacity) from *potestas* (institutional authority), indicating an early analytical separation between **potential power** and **legitimate power**.
> 

> Across Indo-European and non-Indo-European families alike, there is also a recurring pattern in which verbs meaning “to be able” become nouns meaning “power.” This grammatical transformation—from modality to substance—suggests that many linguistic systems conceptualize power not as a static entity but as an **abstracted capability derived from action**. Examples include Greek δύναμαι → δύναμη and Sanskrit śak- → शक्ति.
> 

> In Semitic languages such as Arabic (*قوة*) and Hebrew (*כוח*), the dominant root emphasizes **intensity, strength, or energetic force**, aligning more closely with a physical or energetic ontology of power. Meanwhile, East Asian languages like Chinese (力) encode power in terms of **direct physical force**, without necessarily embedding notions of authority or modality in the same term, thereby maintaining a more concrete semantic core.
> 

> The overarching pattern, therefore, is one of **semantic decomposition followed by selective recomposition**: all languages recognize similar underlying dimensions of power—capacity, force, and authority—but differ in which dimension becomes lexically central and which are treated as secondary or separate constructs. English is unusual in that it collapses these dimensions into a single, highly overloaded term, obscuring distinctions that remain explicit in many other linguistic systems.

## Interpretation

> Power: In social science and politics, power is the social production of an effect that determines the capacities, actions, beliefs, or conduct of actors. Power does not exclusively refer to the threat or use of force (coercion) by one actor against another, but may also be exerted through diffuse means (such as institutions). Power may also take structural forms, as it orders actors in relation to one another (such as distinguishing between a master and a slave), and discursive forms, as categories and language may lend legitimacy to some behaviors and groups over others. / https://www.amazon.com/Sources-Social-Power-History-Beginning/dp/1107635977 / Michael Mann
